While the actor Johnny Depp was being crowned [the sexiest man alive by People magazine](https://www.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-3807272,00.html) yet again, on the other side of the continent his longtime partner, the director [Tim Burton](https://www.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-3587182,00.html), is getting an honor of his own.

Yesterday (Sunday) the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA, or "Moma" as Hebrew spells it) in New York opened [a major retrospective exhibition devoted entirely to Burton and his work](http://www.moma.org/visit/calendar/exhibitions/313). The exhibition, which runs in the city until April 26, 2010, contains more than 700 items connected to Burton's output from his first film, ["Vincent"](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kFZ3gP0pqzE) (1982), to the present day. It is one of the most intriguing exhibitions at the museum this winter season, presenting Burton in all his glory as a filmmaker with a singular visual sensibility. The excitement only grew when the video Burton himself made to promote the exhibition came out.

Burton, 51, is an ideal candidate for an exhibition of this kind. On one hand, he is a film director responsible for some of the biggest hits of the past two decades ("Beetlejuice," "Edward Scissorhands," "Batman," "The Nightmare Before Christmas," "Charlie and the Chocolate Factory" and more). On the other hand, Burton is a singular artist with a distinctive visual style.

From his childhood in California, through art school and later in his films, Burton has been drawn to the gothic, gloomy side of life, while at the same time his work and style are full of childlike, naive touches. He likes to mix death with fantasy and nightmare with magic, and does it all in a package that is painterly and dark but also sugary and babyish. It is no accident that MoMA describes him as a "pop surrealist."

## Growing up, but staying a child

The exhibition devoted to Burton presents his continuous artistic line. Over the years, MoMA has mounted some 80 exhibitions devoted to film artists. The advantage of exhibitions of this kind is that a comprehensive survey makes it possible to discover the director's artistic statement, which sometimes takes years to develop. Burton, in this case, is a double success: on one hand an interesting artist with a clear statement, on the other hand one who appeals to the broad public, which treats the exhibition as a sort of tour of Disneyland.

The exhibition opens with a giant wall presenting Burton's complete filmography, including shelved projects. From there you enter a space designed in the spirit of Burton's characters (with a heavy emphasis on "The Nightmare Before Christmas," which he in fact did not direct). Mainstream fans will be thrilled: the exhibition displays rare memorabilia from films like "Edward Scissorhands" and "Charlie and the Chocolate Factory," with costumes, props and sketches from Burton's own hand.

But beyond the memorabilia, there is a strong emphasis here on his work over the years. The exhibition includes drawings Burton made as a young child, puppets he built (both at art school and later, in preparation for his films), storyboard illustrations he prepared, books he wrote and illustrated and no fewer than dozens of projects that never got off the ground.

In the exhibition Burton is revealed not only as a director but also as a painter, photographer, illustrator and storyteller. Wandering through the place makes it possible to discover the consistency of Burton's style: the attraction to rounded lines, to big eyes, to dark and fragile characters, who perhaps remind him of the rejected child he once was. The exhibition also reveals Burton's maturing, and how he learned to harness his particular style to create cinematic hits.

Here an interesting question arises: precisely while wandering before such dazzling riches, the thought comes up that in fact not much has changed along the

timeline. From Burton's drawings as a boy of 14 to hits like "The Nightmare Before Christmas" there has been development and refinement, but the essentials remain. The gothic/childlike look, the rounded lines and the attempt to fuse horror and soul have been well preserved over 40 years of work.

In fact, one can see that the repetition of content and design styles has not always proven itself, since some of his recent films were too commercial and did not deliver the innovation

and the magic of old. The conclusion is that this is a director who does not always keep an even level in his films, but is always even in his artistic vision.

In any case, whether you look at the exhibition as an art show or as a nostalgic wander through Burton's films, it is a recommended experience. For now it looks like a hit: visitors are flocking, the night tours of the place are packed to capacity, likewise the films accompanying the exhibition and other events connected to the project. No doubt about it, Burton has gotten some not-bad service here for his next film, "Alice in Wonderland," due in theaters in the United States in March 2010.
