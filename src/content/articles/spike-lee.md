Spike Lee, 56, has long since stopped being the biting, kicking filmmaker who supplied the world with angry "joints." The African American director identified with films that carry a political and social message, like Do the Right Thing and Malcolm X, has softened in recent years. The people who work with him say so too, reporting the warm, pleasant atmosphere he creates on set, almost like a favorite uncle.

Still, Lee knows how to bring the groove associated with him, for instance in the new action film he has signed his name to, [Oldboy](https://www.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-4461919,00.html), a new adaptation of Park Chan-wook's classic Korean thriller from 2003, with Josh Brolin (No Country for Old Men) and Elizabeth Olsen (Martha Marcy May Marlene) in the lead roles. The plot follows an alcohol-loving advertising executive (Brolin) who, one drunken night, is kidnapped and locked for twenty years in a room cut off from the world. When he is released, he sets out on a mission to understand why he was imprisoned and, no less important, why he was suddenly set free.

"I don't call it a remake, I call it a reinterpretation," Lee explains. "The Korean film was itself an adaptation. It's based on a Japanese manga story. So the 'original' wasn't original either. Before I took on the project I got the blessing of Park Chan-wook, the director of the Korean version, who told me, 'Make your own film.' I respected the original, but to me the source is really the Japanese manga. I feel like we made a new film here."

Elizabeth Olsen, the film's female lead (and the younger sister of the famous twins Mary-Kate and Ashley Olsen), backs him up: "Why do Romeo and Juliet a hundred times?" she says. "It's a good story. Why not tell it again?"

**There are film buffs and fans of the Korean film who surely don't agree with that argument.**

"My film isn't for them," Lee says. "Whoever thinks Oldboy shouldn't have been made again isn't going to the movies anyway. I loved the story, and I thought it would be amazing to work with Josh Brolin. I did it because I wanted the challenge. I'd never done anything like it. On the other hand, I don't see a big difference between making this film and my other films, even, say, a documentary. It's telling a story. I don't change hats. I stay the same person. That's what makes it easy for me to jump between different projects."

Oldboy was without doubt a challenge for Lee. Beyond the fact that this is a film that had supposedly already been made, there were also logistical problems to deal with, arising from a convoluted plot stretched across a long span of time. "How do you film twenty years of captivity and still make it interesting?" the director says of the main difficulty in the work. "What happens after you get out? We talked about it all the time. I had a lot of meetings, a lot of discussions on the subject."

**How do you think you would feel?**

"I'm sure I'd be in sensory overload. What's an iPhone? What's Shazam? I put those questions in because I want the reality to be real, not cardboard, not a cartoon. I also deliberately don't say where the film takes place. We shot in New Orleans, but the film doesn't reveal which city it is. It could be any city."

Despite the gloomy atmosphere, Olsen reports that behind the scenes it was actually nice. "Spike creates a happy set," she says. "He knows what he wants and gets things done. We'd finish early and laugh all the time."

**You have an especially daring sex scene in there. Did that go lightly too?**

"That one especially. I feel like Spike doesn't much like sex scenes. That's why we did just one take of it, and everything was out in the open because we'd talked about it so much beforehand. He made every effort for me to feel comfortable. Before I could figure out what was happening, it was over." And Lee adds: "If people aren't relaxed, you don't get what you want."

**It's interesting that at a time when there are quite a few films with Black actors in lead roles (12 Years a Slave, The Butler), this time your film doesn't deal with the subject.**

"Listen, every ten years there's the same article, about the awakening of Black cinema. Everyone comes out with the headlines, 'The African Americans have arrived.' And then... there are nine years of drought. History has shown it's either a feast or a desert. How about two years in a row? Is that possible?"

**They say films like 12 Years a Slave have a chance of taking the Oscar this year.**

"Good luck to them. I'm done with the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences (which hands out the Oscars; N.D.). The Academy already had its chance. My film Do the Right Thing lost back then to Driving Miss Daisy. Driving Miss Daisy?! Really, come on. Do the Right Thing is taught in universities. Does anyone care about Miss Daisy today? The Academy messes up sometimes. I stopped getting excited a long time ago."

**Is there any moral you'd like audiences to leave Oldboy with?**

"Not every film has a moral. The audience is supposed to leave with a feeling of 'what the hell was that?'" says Olsen, and Lee joins in: "It's a revenge film. For me it's a film about karma, you reap what you sow. But actually I've stopped answering questions like that. I respect audiences too much. Let everyone feel whatever they want."
