You know hypnotic beauty? The kind that settles on a person and turns heads in every room he walks into? Well, Steve Buscemi has hypnotic ugliness. I apologize in advance to anyone I'm about to offend, but just as every article about [Angelina Jolie](https://xnet.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-3095423,00.html) opens with a description of stunning beauty, I have no choice but to deal with how ugly Buscemi is.

Beauty is a matter of taste, of course, but objectively speaking, Buscemi, 54, has the face of an ostrich, and when he talks it contorts like a gecko after a light microwaving. He probably wouldn't even be offended to read this. After all, the singular looks, plus a talent for stealing the show, have set him up with an excellent career and made him a byword for quality.

He stood out in memorable supporting roles in Reservoir Dogs and Armageddon, and became a Coen brothers favorite in The Big Lebowski, Fargo and others.

On television, too, he stuck to quality productions, playing Tony's cousin for a season on HBO's The Sopranos, for instance. His real jackpot at the network came about two years ago, when he was picked to lead [Boardwalk Empire](https://xnet.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-3091752,00.html), a big-budget production created by Terence Winter (a writer and producer on The Sopranos), Mark Wahlberg and Martin Scorsese, who also directed the pilot.

Buscemi plays Nucky Thompson, a corrupt politician in 1920s Atlantic City who uses Prohibition to get rich smuggling liquor and selling it to the masses. The series took over The Sopranos' slot as the network's prestige crime drama and has collected a pile of awards and praise, including Golden Globes for the show and for Buscemi himself.

To mark the third season's premiere in the United States (and in Israel on the yes Oh channel), Buscemi and I meet at the Crosby, a chic hotel in SoHo. Buscemi looks exhausted and doesn't bother to hide his distaste for journalists. In fact, interviewing him, and trying to get answers out of him, is almost as easy as milking orange juice from a cow.

Not that you can blame him: the journalist before me asked him, in all seriousness, which meal of the day is his favorite (his character holds forth on the subject at the start of the third season), while a French journalist scolded him for "only" acting in the series and not directing any episodes, which makes him "not quality."

Then again, the condescension is a little irritating. After all, he is not Madonna yet. In fact, the character he plays would surely do a better job than he does of buttering up journalists, and would show me more of a good time. When I tell him this, he chuckles.

"I'm glad you like him," he says. "I think a lot of those guys, mobsters like him, were likable. I don't know that I'd want to go out for a night with Al Capone, but I've read he was fun to be around. I guess that's part of who they are: charming sometimes, and sometimes not. I don't think a gangster even tries to file himself under 'good' or 'bad.' He's just who he is."

**Nucky is slipping on the niceness scale. At the end of season two, for instance, he takes out Jimmy Darmody.**

"I think Nucky has been true to himself the whole series. Like he told Jimmy at the end of the pilot: 'Don't underestimate me, I can have you killed.' And he did.

"That last scene with him was hard. I love Michael (Pitt, the actor; N.D.) and I didn't want to see him go, certainly not when I'm the one doing it. Something in me resisted it, and I had to work extra hard to push that down. But I always remember it isn't real. It's a job, and this is what the job asks of me. It's only a character.

"Besides, as the story developed it was obvious it had to be done. If we wanted to stay true to reality, that murder had to happen. You can't wrap a story like this in a sweet, easy-to-swallow coating. It just isn't realistic."

**You've got a reputation of your own for meeting your end on screen. Any chance Nucky dies at some point too?**

"We haven't shot the season finale yet, so I don't know. At least I've already lasted longer than I did on The Sopranos, where I survived one season. Originally I was supposed to be there for two, but in the end they killed me off fast. That was hard, because I really wanted to stay. I fully expect there's a chance they'll kill me here too, and I'm completely fine with it. I'm not afraid to die. I'm used to it by now."

The Boardwalk Empire role, as noted, has helped fill Buscemi's shelf with awards and accolades. So far he has collected a Golden Globe, two Screen Actors Guild awards and two Emmy nominations. While every mention of the show in the press opens with awe at Buscemi's abilities, he tries to keep a low profile.

**Do you still enjoy making the show?**

"Yes. It's a fun job, mainly because of the good writing. As long as Terry (Terence Winter, the creator; N.D.) is involved and hiring good writers and directors, it's still the best job I've ever had. Sure, by the third season you get a little used to it, it's not as exciting as it was at the start, but I'm still having fun. There's hardship too, of course. Burnout. Sometimes it feels like we've been shooting one feature film for three years."

**Three years inside a character this brutal. Doesn't it seep into your private life?**

"It depends how deep the show goes. Sometimes there really are disturbing things. Once in a while an episode stays with me, but mostly it doesn't happen, or I'm not aware of it. Other actors have been very affected in the past and took it home with them, really started behaving differently. I don't keep souvenirs. I take nothing with me. I can drop it in a second, leave the work at work and not carry it around.

"I just don't analyze it. The only thing I usually find strange is that the atmosphere on set is warm, we're laughing and having a good time, and then the cameras roll and we have to step into tense situations and change how we treat each other on the spot."

**Your character is based on a real man, Nucky Johnson. Did you research his life to prepare?**

"I did what I could, but I didn't want to get too hung up on him. I knew going in that we wouldn't be faithful to the original, and that what ended up on screen would be different from what happened in real life. The writers and Terry did most of the research, and the scripts are so detailed that there's nothing left for me to dig into. Sometimes something catches my attention and I go to the computer to read more about it, mostly people from that period.

"The 1920s in America fascinate me. On the one hand, a lot of the issues that preoccupied society then still preoccupy it today, like birth control, or how far the government should reach into private life.

"On the other hand, it's a period of incredible change. On the show I'm fascinated by the changes between 1920 and 1923. Even the clothes changed. I love looking at the wardrobe, and I've started noticing details. Once I stopped a take because in the middle of a scene I suddenly noticed that the extra standing next to me had a tattoo showing on her back. Maybe it wouldn't have shown up on camera, but tattoos weren't accepted back then, so it felt completely out of place to me."

If Buscemi used to be known as "that guy from," thanks to an endless string of supporting roles, Boardwalk Empire has changed that. Ahead of the third season, HBO decided to promote the show by plastering Buscemi's face across the entire city, from giant billboards in the streets to the decoration of subway stations and pay phones. And as we've established, it is not the prettiest mug in town.

"I've gotten used to seeing my face everywhere," says Buscemi, who lives in Brooklyn with his wife, the artist Jo Andres. "I don't notice it anymore. It has brought me more positive reactions, though. A lot more people call me Nucky on the street. Kids are crazy about me too."

**Kids know you by your voice too. Another animated film you voiced, Hotel Transylvania, just came out.**

"Right. Those films are fun to make. I have young nephews, and I love taking them to films I've voiced and answering all their questions about how what they see on screen gets made. I have no problem going from mobster to cartoon. On the contrary, mixing it up is fun. That's what I've tried to do my whole career. I never want to limit myself. I want to try everything and stick with whatever interests me."

**They were needling you earlier about directing. It's a fair point, given that you've directed episodes of The Sopranos, Oz and 30 Rock, and on Boardwalk Empire you don't.**

"I don't want to direct myself! We have very good directors and I lean on them. It's comfortable having good directors; it makes everyone's job easier. I'm perfectly happy to carry on the way things are."

**What about Scorsese? Is he coming back to direct?**

"He hasn't so far, but he knows the door is open. He's just very busy. It seems like he always has 50 projects in the air. And still, it amazes me how involved he manages to stay here too. He approves every storyline, goes over every script, changes things, gives notes. You can feel his touch, even if he isn't actually directing."

**Three seasons in, can you find anything you and Nucky have in common?**

"Yes. Commitment. We both believe that if you want to do something well, you have to commit to it. There's a line on the show: 'You can't be half a gangster.' You can't be half an actor either. You can say a lot of things about him, but the fact is Nucky was deeply committed to his work. We're alike in that we both live by the idea that if you really want something, you have to be all the way in."

**How long do you think you'll stay with the show? Terence Winter said recently that he has material for at least ten more seasons.**

"I don't know. I don't think about it. We're signed for five seasons, but that means nothing. I know Terry is ready, script-wise, to take the story past Prohibition and into the thirties, but as far as I'm concerned it's all good, however long it runs. As long as the quality stays where it is, I'm happy. That's what I'm saying now, of course. I can't know how I'll feel a year from now."

**Winter also said that however many seasons there are, he already knows how the show ends. Would you want to know?**

"Absolutely not! What if we don't shoot the last episode for another seven years? If I know what's coming it'll just drive me crazy."
