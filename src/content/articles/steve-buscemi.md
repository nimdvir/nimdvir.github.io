You know hypnotic beauty? The kind that sits on a person and makes heads turn wherever they walk in? Well, Steve Buscemi has hypnotic ugliness. I apologize in advance if I'm offending anyone, but just as articles about [Angelina Jolie](https://xnet.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-3095423,00.html) open with descriptions of astonishing beauty, I have to address just how ugly Buscemi is.

Beauty is a matter of taste, of course, but objectively it can be seen that Buscemi, 54, has the face of an ostrich, which, when he speaks, twists like a gecko after a light warm-up in the microwave. He probably wouldn't even be offended if he read this. After all, the unique look, plus a talent for stealing the show, set him up with an excellent career and positioned him as a mark of quality.

He stood out in memorable supporting roles in films like Reservoir Dogs and Armageddon, and also distinguished himself as a favorite of the Coen brothers in films like The Big Lebowski, Fargo, and more.

On television, too, he made a point of taking part in quality productions, for instance when he played Tony's cousin for a season on HBO's The Sopranos. His serious jackpot within the network came about two years ago, when he was chosen to lead [Boardwalk Empire](https://xnet.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-3091752,00.html), a big-budget television production created by Terence Winter (who was a writer and producer on The Sopranos), Mark Wahlberg, and Martin Scorsese, who also directed the pilot.

Buscemi plays Nucky Thompson, a corrupt politician in 1920s Atlantic City who exploits Prohibition to get rich smuggling and selling alcohol to the masses. The series took The Sopranos' place in the quality-crime-drama slot and won a slew of awards and praise, including a Golden Globe for the series and for Buscemi himself.

To mark the start of the third season in the United States (and here on yes Oh), Buscemi and I meet at the chic Crosby hotel in SoHo, New York. Buscemi looks exhausted and makes no effort to hide his distaste for journalists. In fact, interviewing him and trying to get answers out of him is a job about as easy as milking orange juice from a cow.

Not that you can blame him: the journalist before me seriously asked him what his favorite meal of the day is (his character talks about the subject at the start of the third season), while a French journalist scolded him for the fact that he "only" acts in the series and doesn't direct any episodes, which makes him "not quality."

On the other hand, his condescension is a little annoying. After all, he isn't Madonna yet. In fact, compared to him, the character he plays in the series would surely know how to butter up journalists better, and give me more of a good time. When I tell him this, he chuckles.

"I'm glad you like him," he says. "I think a lot of those guys, mobsters like him, were likable. I don't know if I'd want to go out partying with Al Capone, but I've read that he was a guy who was fun to be around. I suppose that's part of who they are, sometimes charming and sometimes not. I don't think a gangster tries to categorize himself as 'good' or 'bad' at all. He's just who he is."

**Nucky is going downhill on the niceness scale. At the end of the second season, for instance, he eliminates Jimmy Darmody.**

"I think Nucky was true to himself throughout the series. As he told Jimmy at the end of the pilot: 'Don't underestimate me, I can have you killed.' And he did.

"The last scene with him was hard. I love Michael (Pitt, the actor; N.D.) and didn't want to see him go, certainly not when I'm the one doing it. I had an inner resistance to it, and I had to work especially hard to suppress it. But I always remember it isn't real. It's a job, and it's what I'm required to do. It's just a character.

"Besides, as the plot developed it was clear it had to be done. If we wanted to stay true to reality, that murder had to happen. We can't wrap a story like this in a sweet, easily digestible coating. It just isn't realistic."

**You also have a reputation as someone who often meets his death in various roles. Is there a chance Nucky will die at some point in the series too?**

"We haven't shot the last episode of the season yet, so I don't know. At least I've already lived longer than on The Sopranos, where I survived one season. Originally I was supposed to be there for two seasons, but in the end they killed me quickly. That was hard, because I really wanted to stay. I totally feel there's a chance they'll kill me here too, and I'm really fine with it. I'm not afraid to die, I'm used to it by now."

The role in Boardwalk Empire, as mentioned, helped fill Buscemi's shelf with awards and accolades. He has already collected one Golden Globe, two Screen Actors Guild awards, and two Emmy nominations. While every media reference to the show opens with admiration for Buscemi's abilities, he tries to keep a low profile.

**Do you still enjoy making the series?**

"Yes. It's a fun job, mainly thanks to the good writing. As long as Terry (Terence Winter, the creator; N.D.) is involved and hires good writers and directors, it stays the best job I've ever had. Yes, in the third season you get a bit used to it, it isn't as exciting as at the start, but I still have fun. There's difficulty too, of course, burnout. It sometimes feels as if we've been shooting a feature for three years."

**Being inside such a cruel character for three years, doesn't it seep into your private life?**

"It depends how deep the show goes. Sometimes there really are disturbing things. Occasionally there's an episode that stays with me, but it usually doesn't happen, or I'm not aware of it. Other actors have been very affected in the past and took it home with them, really behaved differently. With me there are no souvenirs. I don't take anything. I'm able to leave it in an instant, to leave the work at work and not take it with me.

"I just don't analyze it. The only thing that's usually strange to me is that there's a warm atmosphere on set, we laugh and have fun, and then the cameras come on and we have to go into tense situations and immediately change how we treat each other."

**Your character is based on a real person named Nucky Johnson. Did you research his life to prepare for the part?**

"I did what I could, but I didn't want to get too attached to him. I knew in advance that we wouldn't be faithful to the original, and that what's shown in the series would differ from what happened in real life. The writers and Terry did most of the research, and the scripts are so detailed that there's nothing left for me to research. Sometimes something catches my attention and I go to the computer to read more about it, mainly about people from that period.

"The 1920s in the United States fascinate me. On the one hand, many of the issues that occupied society then still occupy it today, like the issue of contraception, or of government involvement in private life.

"On the other hand, it's a period of amazing change. In the series I'm fascinated to see the changes between 1920 and 1923. Even the clothes changed. I really love looking at the wardrobe, and I've started noticing details. For example, I once stopped a shot because in the middle of a scene I suddenly noticed that the extra standing next to me had a visible tattoo on her back. Maybe it wouldn't have shown on camera, but since tattoos weren't acceptable at the time, it felt very out of place to me."

If in the past Buscemi was known as "that guy from" thanks to an endless chain of supporting roles, Boardwalk Empire changed the situation. Ahead of the third season, HBO decided to promote the series by smearing Buscemi's face all over the city, from giant billboards in the streets to decorating subway stations and public phones. And as we recall, it's not the best-looking mug in town.

"I've gotten used to seeing my face everywhere," says Buscemi, who lives with his wife, the artist Jo Andres, in Brooklyn. "I don't notice it anymore. It has increased the positive reactions toward me. A lot more people call me Nucky in the street. Kids love me too."

**Kids also know you by your voice. Another animated film you did a voice for, Hotel Transylvania, came out recently.**

"Right. It's fun to make those films. I have young nephews, and I love taking them to films I've voiced and answering all their questions about how what you see on screen is made. I have no problem going from a mobster to a cartoon character. On the contrary, it's fun to vary things. That's what I've tried to do throughout my career. I never want to limit myself. I want to try everything and stay with what interests me."

**They were bugging you earlier about directing. It makes sense, considering you've directed episodes of The Sopranos, Oz, and 30 Rock in the past, and on Boardwalk Empire you don't.**

"I don't want to direct myself! We have very good directors and I depend on them. It's comfortable when there are good directors, it makes everyone's work easier. It's completely fine with me to carry on as now."

**What about Scorsese? Is he coming back to direct on the series?**

"For now he hasn't come back, but he knows the door is open. He's just very busy. It seems like he always has 50 projects in the air. And still, it amazes me how involved he manages to be here too. He approves all the storylines, goes over all the scripts, changes things, makes comments. His touch is very much felt, even if he doesn't direct in practice."

**After three seasons, can you find common ground between you and Nucky?**

"Yes. Commitment. We both believe that if you want to do something well, you have to commit to it. We say in the series, 'You can't be half a gangster.' You also can't be half an actor. You can say a lot of things about him, but the fact is that Nucky was very committed to his work. We're similar in that we both live by the approach that if you really want something, you need to be completely inside it."

**How long do you think you'll continue with the series? Terence Winter said recently that he has material for at least ten more seasons.**

"I don't know, I don't think about it. We're signed for five seasons, but that means nothing. I know Terry is ready, script-wise, to take the plot out of Prohibition and into the 1930s, but as far as I'm concerned it's all good, however long it lasts. As long as the quality stays the same, I'm happy. That's what I'm saying now, of course. I can't know how I'll feel in a year."

**Winter also said that no matter how many seasons there are, he already knows how the series will end. Would you want to know what happens at the end?**

"Absolutely not! What happens if we don't shoot the last episode for the next seven years? If I know what's going to happen it'll just drive me crazy."
