It may be a cliché, but it keeps proving itself: living in New York is stressful. For all the city's advantages, there's something here that clouds the calm, a kind of lethal combination of haste-pressure-anxiety-depression that blows through the streets like a cold wind at the end of September.

After a lunch of grumbling with my friend Luis, a local fashion designer and serial worrier, we decided to go to an introductory class in Buddhism and meditation at the Chakrasambara Kadampa center (I swear that's the name, Chakrasambara Kadampa, and even with a gun pointed at my head I couldn't say it properly). It's free, it's enriching, it's calming, what is there to lose?

We arrived on Sunday morning at the fifth floor of an ordinary building in the Chelsea neighborhood. From the outside the center looks like just another office, but inside the atmosphere is pure ashram: a very large space full of burning candles that light the place with a soft glow, chubby Buddha statues everywhere, meditation cushions on the floor, dozens of chairs arranged for a lecture and a stage with a microphone and a spot for the speaker.

After the obligatory removal of shoes, Luis and I sat down on the chairs and started scanning the crowd. The place was full of single men and women sitting alone in a spiritual atmosphere. Luis immediately marked an African-American woman sitting behind us who was picking at her toes, a very impolite act in his opinion.

After a few minutes the morning's speaker took the stage, a beautiful woman with a medium-to-heavy Russian accent named Natalia Drilova (again, not making this up), and said she was about to open with a guided meditation and then a talk on the important subject: "Where is happiness."

It could have been very nice and enriching, especially considering that the crowd flowed naturally into the meditation. The only pity was that Luis, who by this point was already exhausted from furiously tracking the toe-picking African-American woman, started to giggle. Then to laugh. And laugh. And then I started laughing too. And before we noticed, the light laughter had turned into a crazed laughing fit bordering on an epileptic seizure. At this point we could not stop ourselves. Everything was insanely funny: the crowd, the place, Natalia's accent, the subject of the talk.

Our laughter was embarrassing, but we couldn't stop. While Natalia was trying to guide the crowd to concentrate on breathing through the nose, we lay down on the floor, tears streaming, roaring with laughter and unable to hold ourselves together. It was so unpleasant that I would have gotten up and left, if only I had been able to get up.

And then came the climax: Luis let one rip. Needless to say, this was the moment when three things happened. One, if there had been any chance we would stop laughing, it was now lost. Two, our embarrassment rose to new heights. And three, the room started to smell like a nuclear waste site on a hot summer day. Luckily, Natalia turned out to be a very good sport. "It's good to laugh," she told the crowd. "Laughter is good for your health. Let's all laugh." And then everyone laughed, which finally helped calm us down a little.

After that it was actually an interesting class, whose conclusion is that happiness is inside us all the time, we just need to know how to release it. Next week I'm definitely coming to another class, but this time without Luis. With all due respect, you can't take him anywhere serious.
