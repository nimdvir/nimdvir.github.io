It's a cliché, but it keeps proving itself: New York is a stressful place to live. For all the city's advantages, something here muddies the calm, a lethal blend of rush-stress-anxiety-depression that blows through the streets like a cold wind at the end of September.

After a lunch spent grumbling with my friend Louis, a local fashion designer and serial worrier, we decided to try an introductory Buddhism and meditation class at the Chakrasambara Kadampa center (I swear that's the name, and even with a gun to my head I couldn't pronounce it right). It's free, it's enriching, it's calming. What's there to lose?

We arrived on a Sunday morning at the fifth floor of an ordinary building in Chelsea. From the outside the center looks like one more office; inside, it's as ashram as it gets: a very large room full of burning candles that bathe the place in a soft glow, chubby Buddha statues everywhere, meditation cushions on the floor, dozens of chairs set out lecture-style, and a stage with a microphone and a spot for the speaker.

After the obligatory shoe removal, Louis and I took two chairs and started scanning the crowd. The place was full of single men and single women, each sitting alone in a spiritual frame of mind. Louis immediately zeroed in on an African-American woman behind us who was picking at her toes, an act he found deeply rude.

A few minutes later the morning's speaker took the stage, a beautiful woman with a medium-to-heavy Russian accent named Natalia Drilova (again, not making this up), and said she would open with a guided meditation and then move on to a talk on the important question: "Where Is Happiness."

It could have been very pleasant and enriching, especially since the crowd slid naturally into the meditation. The only pity was that Louis, by this point exhausted from furiously tracking the toe-picking African-American woman, started to giggle. Then to laugh. And laugh. And then I started laughing too. Before we knew it, the light laughter had turned into a deranged laughing fit bordering on an epileptic seizure. By this stage we couldn't stop ourselves. Everything was insanely funny: the crowd, the room, Natalia's accent, the subject of the talk.

Our laughter was embarrassing, but we couldn't stop. While Natalia tried to guide the room to concentrate on breathing through the nose, we lay on the floor, tears streaming, roaring with laughter, unable to hold ourselves together. It was so unpleasant that I would have walked out, if only I'd been able to stand.

And then came the climax: Louis let one slip. Needless to say, three things happened at that moment. One, if there had been any chance of our stopping, it was gone now. Two, our embarrassment reached new heights. And three, the room started to smell like a nuclear waste site on a hot summer day. Luckily, Natalia turned out to be a very good sport. "It's good to laugh," she told the room. "Laughter is good for your health. Let's all laugh." So everyone laughed, which finally helped settle us down a little.

After that it was actually an interesting class, and its conclusion was that happiness is inside us all the time; we just have to know how to let it out. Next week I'm definitely going to another class, but this time without Louis. With all due respect, you can't take him anywhere serious.
