Jamie Foxx is on top of the world. And why wouldn't he be? Since picking up the coveted statuette at the last Oscars for his lead in Ray, Foxx has settled into the fast lane toward Hollywood's club of perks and inside deals. Sitting, happy as a clam, in a suite at the ultra-luxurious Mandarin Oriental in Miami, where he is staying for the shoot of the big-screen Miami Vice, Foxx is enjoying his new image as the fresh prince of Bel-Air. One minute he's showing off a giant diamond watch that looks as if it was built on the ruins of a mine in Ivory Coast ("I got it for free, to wear to some ceremony"). A little later he pulls personal photos out of his pocket, shirtless ones. "Look at the muscles," he says, thrilled. "It's all real, really." And finally, to shore up his standing as a superstar, he makes sure to garnish every other answer with some lethal name-dropping ("So I'm in the car with Kanye, Kanye West"). Any other star in this condition would have had a slap coming, the kind that knocks the swagger loose. With Jamie Foxx, of all people, you find yourself wanting to be generous. Good guy, you know.

**Jamie, life is good, huh?**

"Tell me about it," he grins. "It's hard, so hard being a movie star. Honestly, what's been happening lately is pretty amazing, especially for someone who came up from the bottom. I keep finding myself in completely surreal situations. Like when Will Smith threw a party and I went, and at some point he and Tom Hanks cornered me and started grilling me about how it felt to get the Oscar. Or tour buses suddenly pulling up outside my house, and the guide yells into the microphone 'And this is Jamie Foxx's house,' and a million Japanese tourists pour out in hysterics so they can get their picture taken by the door.

"And there's one more thing, and it's a little ridiculous, but now, when I finally have money, I don't have to pay for anything. A few weeks ago, say, they gave me three Cadillacs. Three cars, you get that? So I passed one on to my sister. She cried, she was so moved."

**In earlier interviews you talked about how hard it was to find love. No need to worry about you on that front anymore either?**

"Let's say that since the Oscar I've been having a lot more sex. And it comes with some crazy moments. Like, there was this woman I was sleeping with, and right in the middle of everything, between rounds, she suddenly moans, 'Oh Jamie, oh Jamie, oh... Academy Award winner Jamie Foxx!!!' The girls in this town, I'd better not get started."

The reason Foxx is airing himself out in public and leaking classified secrets from the bedroom is the round of interviews for his new film, Stealth, which opens in Israel this week, less than two weeks after its American premiere, where it was crowned one of the conspicuous flops of the summer. Audiences, it seems, have had their fill of action. As befits a summer movie, this is a well-oiled production stuffed with explosions and stunts. Foxx plays an elite fighter pilot, one of a trio of stars that also includes the slab of muscle Josh Lucas (Sweet Home Alabama) and the saccharine bombshell Jessica Biel (7th Heaven). The film opens as the trio takes delivery of a stealth jet with souped-up artificial intelligence, which they put to work on secret missions for the US Air Force. When lightning hits the jet's systems it develops a will of its own, and the pilots are dragged into chases around the world trying to head off some lethal embarrassments. Foxx reports that he enjoyed playing a guy with an edge to him: "I play the pilot Henry Purcell, a guy who's a little cocky, a little superior, a little bit of a smart-ass. I based him on real life: I got to meet a few pilots before the shoot, and every one of them had that swagger in his eyes."

**In Israel, too, pilots come with a whiff of stardom. How do you explain it?**

"I think there's something about them that demands attention, because they're taunting death almost daily, and that puts them in a kind of godlike state. I think the cockiness gets even stronger on an aircraft carrier like the one in the film, because there are 9,000 people on board whose whole job is to support a relatively small group of pilots. In a way the pilots are like celebrities. Everybody's looking at them."

**The film makes a lot of the brotherhood between the fliers. Were you a tight bunch during the shoot too?**

"Honestly, I barely saw the other two. Since most of the film is very sophisticated computer effects, the work itself was mostly solitary. We spent most of the shoot in separate rooms, each of us sitting alone in a cockpit hanging 20 meters up in front of a green screen. I saw Josh and Jessica on little video monitors, but during filming I had almost no contact with them. Still, we got to hang out a lot in Australia, where most of the sets were. I can say we put a lot of energy into the work, and about the same amount into celebrating."

The shoot may have been a party, but now that it's time to sell the film, a few ego issues are souring the mood: it turns out Josh Lucas was originally meant to be the star, since Foxx joined the production right after wrapping Ray, which is to say pre-fame. To the great sorrow of Lucas, who had hoped Stealth would be his big break, Foxx walked into the film a small star and walked out a big one, and the marketing and publicity push swung his way (and that despite the fact that Foxx actually has a smaller part than the other two). Director Rob Cohen explains why Lucas got nudged aside: "When Foxx got to the set, nobody knew what was going to happen with Ray. Believe it or not, the part was originally supposed to be even smaller, but we expanded it after we met Jamie and realized how much talent he had. Later, once he'd become a huge star, we thought about giving him more room in the edit, and in the end we decided to stick with the original script. The only change we made was in the marketing, where we put Jamie front and center, like redoing the poster to give him more prominence."

Josh Lucas tries to hide the disappointment and doesn't quite pull it off. "What can you do, my name doesn't pull a crowd the way Foxx's does," he says, deflated. "I'm just glad to be in films people actually get to see." To which there's only one thing to say: don't let them fool you.

Back to Foxx. Nobody expects Stealth to be anything more than another action movie, but Foxx, of all people, is trying to inject a drop of philosophy into the thing: "What we tried to show is that at some point pilots turn into players in a very sophisticated video game. They forget they're dropping bombs and just think about the flying, so it becomes something purely mathematical. You could say the military today is becoming more and more of a video game. In my opinion, in 10 years there won't be any pilots. Everything will be run by machines."

**How well do you understand now what it really takes to fly a plane?**

"I think quite a bit. The advantage is that through acting you get to look at other people's lives, and after the shoot you feel like you've tried on a life you didn't choose for yourself. As an actor you basically multiply your life: say you've got 50 years of adulthood, working in films stretches that."

For all the confidence on display now, Foxx is the first to admit that the landing in the cockpit didn't come easy. After a modest upbringing at his grandparents' house (he moved there after his parents divorced), he tried his luck with a solo album called Peep This. Prodded by a friend to develop his comic talent, he started performing at local stand-up clubs, moved on to television, and eventually landed a show of his own on the WB, The Jamie Foxx Show. It took a few more parts in films of varying mediocrity before the real breakthrough, which came in 2004 with two hits, Collateral opposite Tom Cruise and Ray. Both, by the way, earned him Oscar nominations at the last ceremony.

Foxx knows about the new status. "I have power now," he smiles, "but I think of it as a kind of animal that needs taming. There are times when the animal wakes up, and then I have to send it back to its corner, remind myself who I am and where I came from. I only let it out when it can help, say when I need to walk into a room and impress somebody to get a part. Then I walk in with the pose: 'Jamie Foxx is in the house!'"

**So with the animal in your pocket, what projects have you lined up?**

"Since then I've been working on a few new films you'll be hearing about, the current one being Miami Vice, which is shooting right now. I'm also still doing comedy, so if I ever want a break from acting I can always go back to the stand-up clubs. If I do go back to stand-up it'll definitely be different, because the Oscar changed me as a comedian. Until I made it, my whole act was laughs on the way up, but what do I do now? Fuck it, I finally have money! So now I make fun of Hollywood, that kind of thing. Besides that, I'm working on a new solo album, with Kanye, which will include, among other things, a song about that crazy Oscar night."

**Since we've spent half the interview on a statue, maybe you can close by telling us which room you keep it in?**

"Honestly, it's at my agent's. You see, my house is like a neighborhood, friends and family coming and going all the time. When the Oscar was at my place they kept clowning around with it, tossing it back and forth, touching it, because it's kind of a thing. Now my manager is holding it so nothing happens to it. I keep a fake Oscar at home for the guests."
