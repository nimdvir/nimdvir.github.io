The monster conceived by the writer Mary Shelley in her classic novel Frankenstein, from the beginning of the 19th century, has been given another incarnation in the effects-laden action film "[I, Frankenstein](https://www.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-4480590,00.html)," made by the director Stuart Beattie. Instead of the lumbering, slow-witted creature presented to us in the past in horror films old and new, this time we meet him as a sharp, agile, muscular hero. Even good-looking. The American actor Aaron Eckhart was chosen to play him, and in the film he is dragged into a struggle between supreme beings: the gargoyles and the demons.

"In earlier films the creature was very big and lumbering, heavy and slow," Eckhart admits in an interview with ynet. "Since this is an action film, he needs to be fast and agile and lethal. He needs to think fast, and he needs to learn fast and adapt, and the film is much more dynamic that way, and he also takes his shirt off."

And with the shirt coming off there is naturally also someone who is supposed to be tempted, and in I, Frankenstein that is the character of the scientist Terra Wade, who tries to help the monster help all of humanity in the last-ditch battle between the queen of the gargoyles (Miranda Otto) and the leader of the hostile demons (Bill

Nighy). The gentle Wade is played by the Australian actress Yvonne Strahovski, known to us from the series Dexter and Chuck and from the action film Killer Elite. "It's definitely possible there's sexual tension between the two characters," she says. "Once she understands he's like a human being, there's that curiosity."

According to Eckhart, the source of Frankenstein's rage in the film is the feeling of rejection he experienced in the past: "Where does this action come from? It comes from the rage of a child who wasn't loved by his parents," he explains. "The story of this creature's birth, his creation, the father who rejected him and the society that shunned him. He felt no love and was treated badly by society. I think there are millions in our world who feel the same as our hero, and those feelings draw out rage. And so I think this film is very current."
