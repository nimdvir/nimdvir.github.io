The next surprising discovery came right at the start of Ryan Gosling's interview ahead of his new film, The Place Beyond the Pines. The star, who plays a professional motorcycle rider who turns to crime to support his son, wanted to know first of all how Israel's Ofnobank was doing. "He was good, wasn't he?" he asks with a smile, and declares: "He's my idol. I've read a lot about him."

And indeed, Gosling showed an impressive command of the life story of Roni Leibovitz, and heaped praise on him in front of everyone. "I love the Ofnobank because we think alike," he says. "Even before I heard about him, I'd thought up an excellent theory for robbing banks: you arrive on a motorcycle, you rob with the helmet on so nobody can identify you, and then you flee and hide inside a truck, so the police don't spot you and keep looking for the motorcycle rider," he explains. "I walked around with that thought for many years."

*Roni Leibovitz. Spot the differences (Photo: Meir Partush)*

It sounds a little strange, but there's a chance that the inspiration Gosling took from the Israeli Ofnobank is what ultimately brought him the role in the film, directed by Derek Cianfrance, who made Blue Valentine, in which Gosling starred alongside Michelle Williams. "One day I was having dinner with Derek, and he asked me what I'd most like to do in life," the Hollywood hunk recalls. "I told him that if I could really do whatever I wanted, I'd want to rob a bank, but I'm afraid of going to prison.

"And then he said to me, 'Hey, I'm writing a script about exactly that.' So I told him about the method I'd invented, and together we developed the script and how the robberies would play out in it. All the ideas came from me, inspired by the Ofnobank, whom I'd read about before I ever started imagining how a robbery would go."

**And like the Ofnobank, do you know how to ride a motorcycle too?**

"Yes, but not at the level of the chases and stunts in the film. In some of the scenes it really is me, and for that I spent a few months learning professional riding with Rick Miller (the stuntman from The Dark Knight films). At first the idea was that I'd do all the scenes, but I quickly understood that just as you need to practice shooting hoops from the age of six to play in the NBA, I wasn't going to turn into the Ofnobank in a few months either."

## Who's the Real Man?

The Place Beyond the Pines is a film a little deeper than plain action. It's also, or in fact mainly, a film that deals with masculinity, violence, family, despairing America, the failing education system, and plenty more painful sides of American culture, all rolled together. "The bank robbery isn't the main thing for my character," Gosling says. "The main thing is coping with the question of how you deal with another man raising your child.

"We created a character full of contradictions who tries to answer the question of what makes a man a man. On the one hand he has tattoos and motorcycles, and then they bring him a baby, and he realizes that all the things that filled his life aren't what really makes the man."

**But still, the solution he finds to the problem involves violence.**

"I feel that a lot of films make a fetish of violence, they make it cool. We didn't want to do that. Yes, it's a story of violence, but not a celebration of violence. It's a film about America, or the way I see it, a film about American tribes, about how our society is a collection of traditions and you're actually born into choices that people made before you."

*Ryan Gosling. A film about America (Photo: Gettyimages)*

To be connected as closely as possible to the real America, the film's production did everything it could to put the actors in the atmosphere. "We shot the whole film in Schenectady, New York, which really is the city where the story takes place," Gosling says. "We wanted reality. They introduced us to local police officers and even to someone who had once robbed a bank in that very city and gone to prison for it. It was very interesting. He told us what led him to it, how he felt while doing it, about the thrill and about repeating the act again and again. Even in the scene itself, everyone there except me, the tellers, the customers, even the guards, are mostly real people who had been present at a robbery like that in the past."

**You brought people back to their past traumas. How did that go?**

"Honestly, at first it was a failure, but not because of trauma. On the first attempt, I burst into the bank to carry out the robbery and suddenly I see that one of the women behind the counter, instead of getting into the role and being frightened, is simply filming me on her phone. In other cases people started laughing at me in the middle. They kept messing up. Until by the 15th take I was so angry and exhausted that I went into the bank screaming for real, gun drawn, and genuinely scared them. And then it happened. Suddenly the robbery looked real."

Another touch of reality in the film is that Gosling's partner and the mother of his son is played by Eva Mendes, who is also his partner in real life. In fact, he was the one who brought her on board. "In general there's a difference between a film and reality. It's like robbing a bank. I didn't really do it. The baby they gave Eva and me in the film was really cute. I really loved him. If they'd brought him to me, I might have raised him. When there's a baby on the scene, your instincts kick in. You definitely feel something."

**Is it an advantage or a disadvantage to work with people you know?**

"Absolutely an advantage. There's intimacy and history here, not only with Eva. I'd worked with Derek before too, and I've known him for years. That matters to me in life. That's how I work. My personal manager, my lawyer, my publicist, my accountant, they're all people who have been with me for many, many years."

When Gosling talks about many years he isn't exaggerating. He was born in Canada and at a young age moved with his family to Hollywood to make use of the cute face and be a child actor. When he grew up he managed the transition to the adult world and starred in films like The Notebook, Half Nelson, The Believer, and more. At the same time he began starring on lists of Hollywood's sexiest men, and today he is considered one of the hottest guys in the industry.

**You managed to survive the transition from child actor to the grown-up world.**

"I actually think it helped me. It kept me on the ground. I can't hide my embarrassments. You can google me and see my past. Sometimes I wish I could erase it, but in the end I'm glad, because I don't take myself seriously. I know I once looked ridiculous."

## Trying to Get Out of the Abs

Although he wants to grow up and become a serious actor, these days Gosling is Hollywood's hot kid, mainly because of the six-pack, which he tries to disown. "It's not real, it's Photoshop. Kidding. A little Photoshop and a lot of work at the gym," he jokes, and declares that he has no intention of taking his shirt off in his next films, to the disappointment of his many female fans and of the journalists at People magazine and elsewhere who pick him every year for the list of the sexiest men on earth.

"I've been in a lot of films and didn't take my shirt off in them, but everyone latched onto a few isolated scenes I did, and a snowball started. Suddenly I felt I was limiting myself, so I won't be doing it again. I won't undress in a film anymore. I'm very careful to stay varied and very wary of typecasting. I'm aware that a problem is starting here, so I'm deliberately taking different roles, ones based on the character, and not taking my shirt off anymore."

Gosling much prefers the praise he gets when Hollywood regularly compares him to giant stars like Brad Pitt and George Clooney. The latter even worked with him on The Ides of March, as an actor and as a director.

And today Gosling, too, wants to move to the other side of the camera, and is in fact currently directing a film that will deal with the sex clubs of New York. "George makes everything look easy, so it isn't," he says. "I've never directed, and it's a long, complicated process that's still ahead of me."

So although he intends to explore the big city's sex scene as a director, he insists on staying dressed from now on, except that, like the strippers in his future film, it turns out he too is willing to take his clothes off for the right sum. "We'll leave it open," he says, backing away from the sweeping declaration. "The question is how much they'll be willing to pay. For the right sum there's a chance you'll not only see me without a shirt, I'll do it while singing and dancing."
