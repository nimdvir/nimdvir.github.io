On paper Karim Rashid is a leading industrial designer, somewhere alongside Philippe Starck and Marcel Wanders (Time magazine even crowned him the most influential designer in America). In practice, much like Irit Rahamim, the TV decorator, or the Facebook page of Miri Regev, the culture minister, Rashid actually thinks of himself as a designer of atmosphere. As such, he knows full well that God is not in the small details but in the overall vibe, in creating an experience greater than the sum of its furniture. We meet at his studio in Manhattan to talk about his new design project, [the Poli House boutique hotel](https://www.atmag.co.il/%d7%a2%d7%99%d7%a6%d7%95%d7%91-%d7%95%d7%90%d7%93%d7%a8%d7%99%d7%9b%d7%9c%d7%95%d7%aa/%d7%a2%d7%99%d7%a6%d7%95%d7%91/%d7%9e%d7%a2%d7%a6%d7%91%d7%99%d7%9d/%d7%a7%d7%90%d7%a8%d7%99%d7%9d-%d7%a8%d7%90%d7%a9%d7%99%d7%93-%d7%9e%d7%a2%d7%a6%d7%91-%d7%90%d7%aa-%d7%9e%d7%9c%d7%95%d7%9f-the-poly-house-%d7%91%d7%aa%d7%9c-%d7%90%d7%91%d7%99%d7%91), which opened recently in central Tel Aviv. "My ultimate dream is really to design a planet," he laughs. "What I'd most like is to start a new world and design everything in it. Realistically, in recent years I've been interested in designing buildings because that's the closest thing to it. But it's unpredictable work: when you plan a space, you can never know what the final result will be. You can know what color the walls will be or how the rooms will be arranged, but there's an emotional, atmospheric component you can never predict. My goal is to create a unique energy, and I'm always on edge about whether it'll arrive."

**And at the Poli House, did you succeed?**

"I think so, but don't ask me. You have to be there for the experience. The pictures don't do the place justice."

**How did you even come to design a hotel in Tel Aviv?**

"I got an email out of nowhere and with very good timing. I was planning to come to Israel to give a lecture anyway, so I met with the developers. I really liked the building, a Bauhaus from 1937. I saw right away that there was energy there."

**Did you like the city? Israeli design?**

"Yes, I know the city relatively well, I've walked around it a lot. I was very impressed by the Sarona compound, for example, I thought they did a successful job there. What I liked about Tel Aviv is the combination of a small place with the atmosphere of an urban capital. I love the combination of intimacy and globality, and I tried to capture it in the hotel too."

## Rashid-land

Rashid seems to have carefully produced the experience of meeting him. The interview takes place in his large studio in Manhattan's Hell's Kitchen neighborhood, adjoining the impressive private house where he lives with his wife and his three-and-a-half-year-old daughter. Two things stand out immediately: one, that this is apparently a wealthy man (a private house plus a huge studio in the heart of Manhattan is a matter of some tens of millions of dollars). And two, that this is a kingdom that is entirely Rashid-land. Everything in the place was designed by Rashid, but really everything, from the pink sofas at the entrance (his favorite color and his trademark), through the futuristic bathrooms to the water bottles and the tape dispensers on the desks. Everything in pink and white, in a poppy, glossy and at times almost trashy style. In the open space on the ground floor young designers sit at personal workstations wrapped in neon lights, toiling over the various projects Rashid is involved in around the world. Scattered around them are glass cabinets with a selection of his best-known works, which gives the place the feel of a mini-museum: spacey bottles he designed for Pepsi, a wastebasket that sold millions of units, cosmetics and perfumes for Kenzo and Estée Lauder and even manhole covers and sneakers. His own workstation is one floor up and overlooks everything. He turns up an hour late for the interview, even though he lives next door, and still, when he finally arrives, it's clear this is a seasoned star who knows how to conquer effortlessly with charm and a killer look. He is dressed entirely in white, from the shoes of his own design to the nails painted with polish, covered in elegant tattoos and keeping up a figure that makes him look at least a decade younger than his 56 years. He also turns out to be a fascinating conversationalist who pulls out existential insights at a dizzying pace. For instance, he uses his simple clothing for a social statement that connects to the Poli House hotel as well: "I call the current era the casual age," he says. "Look at how you and I are dressed. People today are tossing aside the heavy formality, the suit and tie have no meaning anymore. It's a kind of uniform that has no place in our day."

**Are you coming out against fashion here?**

"My problem with style in general is that it isn't honest. We used to wear clothes to play a game. Today people are tired of pretending. Casualism has meant that both the CEO and the employees come in a Uniqlo T-shirt and feel comfortable. That's the reality I aspire to, that's the world I hope to help build. Thanks to casualism we can be honest with ourselves and with the other, accept each other as who we are and not the role we're playing. I think the whole world is moving in this direction. The world is going through a period of 'new honesty,' it's an age of transparency that I see in every medium around me. In architecture, for example: everyone builds in glass today. We used to put up concrete walls and lots of partitions, today we try to have as little separation as possible between us and the world. I tried to apply that design honesty at the Poli House as well, it's part of the digital information age we live in today. The hotel, and my work in general, convey connectivity. That's something I'm obsessive about in my work."

**Is that essentially your design agenda?**

"It's something that has been developing in me over the past 20 years. Ever since I got my first computer, an Apple, in the late eighties, I felt the digital age was going to intensify and change us in every sphere of life. It took time, but I think we're now at that point, which is really the start of a new era. I like to say that today we are Bioneers, a combination of Pioneers and Binary. Until now we built the analog world and now we're moving to the digital world."

**Only now?**

"The digital age is only 40 years old, that's not a lot of time. There's still confusion about how to integrate technology into our lives, how to adapt to it. We're still afraid of the future, partly thanks to Hollywood, which likes to show us a dark and gloomy future. I don't think it'll be like that. Everything will keep improving, it'll be better. It's so clear to me what's going to be, and those are the things I feel a need to talk about in my work."

**But if the future is digital, then there's actually no need for your work, because you're all about the tangible, about designing physical products.**

"True, but we'll all be dead long before that happens. In the near future people still need the physical dimension. My agenda is to communicate the digital age through tangibility, to deliver the message of the digital through the analog. I look at the beginning of the world and at the Tower of Babel. We were given different languages then so we couldn't communicate with one another and reach God. And here suddenly we have one language again. We've gone in a giant circle. We're marching again toward one world, a global village, where the walls and borders disappear. It won't happen in our lifetime, but humanity will eventually get there, to the point where we're all one race again. We're at the beginning of that process. It's natural evolution, it's the way we're meant to go. Even the skeptics, the anarchists, the opponents, won't manage to stop this process. There's no choice."

**There are those who would argue the opposite, that the future is a return to the cave age.**

"I don't agree. In my view you shouldn't resist the revolution that's coming. On the contrary, you should cooperate with it as much as possible. If we embrace the digital age we can quickly overcome everything I call 'analog bullshit' that holds us back. Analog bullshit, in my view, is territoriality, religion, wars. These things are going to disappear because the world is going to become completely connected. The connectivity being created is amazing, the digital will eliminate racism. Technology lets us connect and move forward."

## Dirt cheap

**As an ambassador of boundary-breaking connectivity, guess what he thinks of President Donald Trump.**

"Trump symbolizes for me everything that's bad in the world today. He scares me with his semi-racist, quasi-misogynist statements. It's as if a small child was handed enormous power to play with."

**Would you design one of his houses for him? Would you give the White House a refresh?**

"He'd never ask me for something like that, but yes, I'd be happy to redesign the White House. Not for him, for the American people. The White House, as far as I'm concerned, belongs to the Middle Ages. It's exactly the pomp I'm talking about. It's ridiculous to me that in 2017 the White House is all chandeliers and heavy furniture. What's the connection between that and the era we live in? None. To me that's dishonesty." Speaking of others, there's room to pause for a moment for a little self-criticism. For instance, in contrast to the grandeur of the Oval Office, wandering through Rashid's studio raises every so often a slight feeling of cheapness. A thought that crossed my mind more than once about his designs is that a lot of the things recall holiday gifts bought at the Soho gift shop in Dizengoff Center for the aunt from Ra'anana. Surprisingly, Rashid has no problem with the cheap look. On the contrary, he takes pride in it: "The floor here is 100 percent recycled wood that costs 2 dollars a square meter," he boasts. "Nobody can compete with that price. I design with it because it's cheap. No nails, you lay it, boom boom boom, and it's done. My walls are glass or prints I print myself, and fast. Cheap materials and accessible technology represent today for me." Another design agenda is his commitment to the environment. "All my products are 100 percent recycled and biodegradable, no toxic materials. I'm a big believer that designers need to be responsible toward the environment they work in."

**I actually wouldn't have associated your design with sustainability and recycling.**

"Most people make that mistake about me, just because my design is colorful. That's the only reason. Today it's become a hot topic, but I was always like this. I was a child when the wave of environmental awareness began. I grew up in Canada, which is one of the world's leading countries in everything to do with ecological thinking. When I went to university there, it was all we talked about."

Here too he has a bellyful: "What's happening in the design world today in terms of the environment is also, to me, an example of dishonesty," he says. "In lots of restaurants today everything is wood, the tables, the chairs, and then people think 'ooh, nature.' But what they don't know is that the way those trees are processed is very wasteful in energy, or that the materials in those pieces of furniture are far more toxic than plastic. It's a lie. Every time I open a magazine presenting 'ecological furniture' made of wood I get annoyed. People don't know that lots of furniture like that contains terribly toxic, even carcinogenic, materials. On the other hand, the chairs I design are cheap, practical, environmentally friendly, modular and comfortable all at once. And the moment you're tired of them you can grind them up and make something new out of them. Something else that annoys me is the industrial style that's also very fashionable now. In recent months I gave up three big restaurant design projects because they wanted the hanging lamps with the glowing filaments, the metal stools. What's good about that? It's terrible for the environment. Why not use LED lighting instead, which lasts 40 years? It's a fake style, it cheats the consumer. We need to live simple, fast, comfortable lives. Instead of cutting down trees and burning energy to produce furniture."

**Why do you think people don't get that point, given the information revolution?**

"That's also a flaw of the digital age. People today are mostly shallow. We only touch the surface, but don't dig deep. On Facebook, for example, every time I post something serious I get seven likes. But if I put up a silly picture of cats I get 3,000. That shallowness means people can fake an 'ecological thinking' ideology without really understanding what's good for the environment."

**What future are you planning for yourself in your career?**

"I always try to do things I haven't touched before. I've already done hundreds of chairs and tables, I want new things, things that are needed. I want to design products for people with disabilities and special needs, for the older population and for the elderly. I feel it's a market that doesn't get enough attention even though it has enormous potential. All the baby boomers are already past 60. The share of the older population, which I'm starting to count myself part of, is at an unprecedented peak. And since we grew up with cool, innovative products, we want to have them when we age too. My mother is 84, and there's almost nothing cool and well designed on the market for her."

## "A girls' color"

Despite the name, Rashid, born in Egypt, stresses that he feels no connection to the Middle East. "I have no personal connection to the culture and the region, I have no connection to Arab culture. I feel nothing toward it. I left Egypt at age two and spent most of my life in Canada and the United States. Since we left Egypt neither I nor my family has had any connection to the place. My father never went back there, we never spoke Arabic at home. People always ask me if I'm Muslim and I answer that I don't believe in religion. As far as I'm concerned I'm Canadian, that's the country I grew up in, where I went to university and studied design. That's where I absorbed the values that guide me in life."

**Values you're now passing on to your daughter?**

"My daughter is three and a half and I try to spend as much time with her as I can every day. It's convenient that my house is attached to my workplace," he laughs. "My education philosophy is based on two basic principles: the first is 100 percent honesty. She already knows there's no Santa Claus, that there's death and that in the end we'll all grow old and die. I feel the honesty helps her grow up smarter. The second principle is not to fall into clichés, especially when it comes to gender: there are no rules for being a man and a woman, Disney's rules about princes and princesses are bullshit. I don't want her growing up thinking there's a prince who'll come to save her."

**And still, pink is your favorite color.**

"Because pink is a beautiful, positive, amazing color in my eyes. I love it precisely because of the stupid rule that says it's 'a girls' color.' I stick my tongue out at it. I want to break the conventions, and I do it in pink."
