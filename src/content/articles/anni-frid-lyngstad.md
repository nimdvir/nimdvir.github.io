On Monday, November 15, Anni-Frid "Frida" Lyngstad, one quarter of the super-successful group ABBA, will celebrate 65 years of existence on the planet. These have been 65 stormy years that include (take a breath, this is a long one): membership in the most successful pop group in the world (second only to the Beatles); a solo career that rests mainly on that group's success; marriage to bandmate Benny Andersson, including life in a lavish castle, becoming a genuine royal couple and, after three years, a well-publicized divorce; two more husbands, the last of them the German prince Heinrich Ruzzo, thanks to whom she received the title of princess; widowhood, her husband died of cancer; the death of her daughter (from her first husband) in a car accident; moving countries, including living in London and in Switzerland, where she lives today; and a late career as an activist for raising awareness of global warming and the environment, which occupies her these days.

Without a doubt, an impressive output for a woman of 65, but over the stormy life of Anni-Frid (who will be called Frida from here on) hangs one especially large secret: she was born, not to say cynically "created," as the result of a monstrous Nazi project of racial improvement.

The project, called the Fount of Life (Lebensborn Krigsbarnforbundet), was an initiative of Heinrich Himmler, head of the SS, who sought to create a master race of people. Her father, Alfred Haase, was a Nazi soldier stationed in Norway and paired with her mother, Synni, a Norwegian with Aryan features, meaning golden hair and blue eyes.

According to reports, some 12,000 other Norwegian children were born as part of the improvement project. The intentions may have been to improve and refine the race, but fate was neither refined nor improved for most of these children, born at the end of the war (and called in Norway Tyskerbarnas, "the German children").

Because of the dubious circumstances of their birth, the children and their mothers were treated as traitors, aliens and outcasts in post-war Norway. Norwegian society saw them as damaged goods and did not shy from any means to banish them from its midst. "The German children," to whom Frida belonged, were sent to special children's homes, far from the alienating society.

They suffered humiliation, torture and various persecutions. Some were placed in psychiatric institutions, where the government conducted assorted strange experiments on them. A few hundred of these children, today in their sixties and beyond, sued the Norwegian authorities for conducting experiments on them within an institutional framework. The army and the universities did not regard them as equal citizens, and therefore did not hesitate to exploit their power and use the children as guinea pigs for drugs and other substances. The suit was rejected by Norway's Supreme Court because of the statute of limitations.

The Fount of Life organization, which represents the German children and is named after the accursed project, has not given up, and keeps trying to sue Norway at the international court for its war crimes, and even maintains an active website on which it lays out its accusations. A large share of the German children, the organization claims, those who remained in their homeland, were sent to psychiatric institutions and children's homes, from which they were released only in the sixties, outcast and without the ability to cope with society. The children were identified in Norway with Nazi Germany, and were therefore treated as third-class citizens: they were persecuted, various experiments were carried out on them (the central claim is that the Norwegian government tested the drug LSD on them) and they suffered from a lack of basic rights.

Even when they were released from the children's homes or the psychiatric institutions, their suffering did not end: because they had not grown up within society, many were again identified as mentally ill and sent to long, unnecessary hospitalizations. Their mothers also suffered a great deal. They were separated from their children and sent to special camps so as not to mix with society, and there they were put to hard labor. To Frida's (relative) good fortune, she did not belong to the group that remained in Norway and therefore did not suffer the persecution and the torture.

Together with several thousand other German children, she was sent out of Norway immediately after her birth. As part of various deals a large group of the children was sent to different countries around the globe and their identities were changed. Frida and her mother were sent to Sweden, where the mother died of illness after two years and Frida grew up an orphan. The Nazi father, as noted, had returned to his homeland long before and did not raise Frida.

## Father alive

Apart from the sorry circumstances of her birth, life pretty much smiled on her, at least at the start. From a young age Frida knew she was going to be a singer and used to sing at events at her school. At 13 she joined a professional ensemble for the first time, The Evald Eks Orchestra, and with it performed every weekend for a dancing crowd. She went on to win a Swedish talent contest called "New Faces," and so appeared on television in one of the broadcasts that drew the highest ratings in the country's history.

At 17 she married the salesman/musician Ragnar Fredriksson. The two brought two offspring into the world: Hans Ragnar (now 47) and Ann Lise-Lotte, who, as noted, was killed in 1998 in a car accident in the US (and left Frida one grandson, Jonathan). Frida and Ragnar divorced in 1970, and already in the last year of their marriage she met the musician Benny Andersson backstage at a song contest.

The two lived together but married only in 1978, at the height of the success of ABBA, the band that changed the world of pop, which they founded together with another couple, Agnetha and Björn. It was Andersson who pushed Frida to investigate her past and her roots.

Although she knew the circumstances of her birth, Frida was convinced all those years that her father was no longer among the living. Only in 1977, following an article published in a German newspaper, did Frida learn that her father was alive and well and working in Germany as a pastry chef. Under pressure from Andersson, Frida met her father, a meeting she later recounted in several interviews she gave to the media. The German father, surprised to find he had a millionaire pop-star daughter, was brought to Sweden and met Frida at her lavish home. "It was hard," she told the British "Observer." "I can't really connect with him and love him the way I could have if he'd been around when I was growing up."

## A dead daughter

Throughout her life, and especially since meeting her father, Frida suffered from depression and severe moods. She separated from Andersson in 1981, and by 1982 she had already landed in London to try her luck. In 1986 she moved to Switzerland, and there she has stayed to this day. In 1992 Frida married for the third time, to the prince and architect Heinrich Ruzzo, and automatically became a princess herself. The royal life did not last long, since in 1999, just a year after her daughter was killed, Heinrich too died of a cancerous tumor.

Frida stayed in Switzerland and to this day is engaged in activism to promote awareness of global warming and the environment. In singing, Frida never really broke through as a soloist. She released five solo albums, the last of them, "Djupa andetag" ("Deep Breaths"), came out in 1996. She performed until the mid-2000s at various events, and in 2004 released her last song, "The Sun Will Shine Again," recorded for the album of the British musician Jon Lord.

In 2005 she said she would never sing again, and so far she is keeping the promise. Even the longed-for ABBA reunion, ahead of "Waterloo" being named the best song of Eurovision's 50 years, did not happen, in part because of her refusal to take the stage. Beyond the pop career, over the years Frida became a sort of honorary representative for those German children, although she has not spoken much about the affair publicly.

"She achieved amazing things in Sweden," said Tor Brandacher, spokesman for the Fount of Life organization. "If she had stayed in Norway she would have been marked as a 'freak' and would not have managed to integrate properly into society. There's no doubt that by the very fact that Frida succeeded and stayed in the public eye, she managed to remind everyone of the secret the Norwegians would have been happy to forget."
