As of today, most viewers know David Duchovny as Hank Moody, the hard-drinking, sex-loving, quick-tongued writer from the hit series Californication, which begins its third season this week on HOT 3.

The gap between Fox Mulder, the character that made Duchovny famous in The X-Files, and Moody, the character currently helping him pay the bills, is a wide one: where one was buttoned up, disciplined, and preoccupied with fateful questions about the future of humanity, the other is shallower, hedonistic, dissolute, artistic, and charismatic.

The resemblance between the two, if one has to be found, is that both are men who go against the grain, refuse to bow to society's dictates, and pay a price for it. Loneliness, for instance, even though both characters long, openly or secretly, for female closeness.

In real life, Duchovny, 50 by now, is far more Moody than Mulder. Like Moody, a once-successful novelist who moves to Los Angeles and loses the joy of writing, Duchovny has a small writer hiding inside him too. It starts in the genes: he was born in New York in 1960 to a mother who worked in education and a father who was a writer and journalist (and Jewish, with the first name Amram).

He finished his studies at Princeton with a degree in English literature, then went on to Yale, where he completed a master's in the same field. He even began a doctorate on "magic and technology in contemporary poetry and prose," but never finished it. Duchovny has always written and dabbled in poetry, and on finishing his bachelor's degree he won an award from the American society of poets. But his handsome looks led him into modeling work, and from there into acting.

Since the start of his career, Duchovny has zigzagged between two fields: science fiction and sex. He first became known for the lead role in the soft-erotic series Red Shoe Diaries. Then he moved on to Mulder in The X-Files, which brought him enormous worldwide fame. Now, as noted, he's back in sex, with the lead in Californication.

In life, too, he drifts in and out of the world of urges. Although he's married to the actress Téa Leoni and the two have two children, the tabloids reported that Duchovny is no small lover of sex, and that in 2008 he checked himself into a sex-addiction facility.

## A will and a way to shock

In Californication, too, Moody is a decidedly sexual creature, and the series includes no small amount of nudity, sex, and foul language. "We're always thinking about how to balance the ability to shock the audience against being more restrained," Duchovny says in an interview with Maariv.

"On the one hand, we have the ability and the will to shock. On the other, too much can wear the audience out. Sometimes you don't want to shock viewers so as not to ruin the story. It's something we weigh constantly as we move forward with the series. I think what the show keeps coming back to, again and again, is the relationship between Hank and Karen, his mythological beloved. That's really the emotional anchor, and from there you can stray into the more or less 'shocking' bits.

"On top of that, I think one of the reasons we've always managed to slip wild scenes in with some charm is that everyone knows it's a comedy. Even though the actors play the scenes as realistically as they can, nobody doubts there's a comic side here. I think Tom Kapinos, the creator of the series, managed to find the right balance, and we, the actors, try to translate it into performance. It's hard, and you can miss sometimes, but that's part of the fun too."

**Were there things in the script you refused to do?**

"Oh, yes. Yes. You know, there were times I told the production that it's one thing to write it, but something else entirely to put your face on it. I think Tom understands me, and there are times we have a creative discussion about it. We respect each other."

There's no small amount of irony to be found in Duchovny's life. On the one hand, he appears in one of the most sexual series on television today. On the other, he was publicly crucified when reports began about his fondness for sex in his personal life. On a third hand, he has declared in interviews that he "is not a sex addict," and claimed to be a model family man. The truth, as in the eternal motto of The X-Files, is . . . somewhere.

Duchovny doesn't like talking about the subject, and doesn't feel any need to be an ambassador for free love. "It's not on my agenda to make America sexually liberated," he says. "True, I'm in a series with a lot of sex in it, but our agenda is to tell stories in a comic way, and bring in some feeling too. We're a strange hybrid, an emotional comedy. We have no intention of influencing the American worldview on sex. It's there, but to entertain and amuse, not to educate and change."

Still, it's hard to ignore the resemblance between Duchovny and Moody, whether in the love of writing or in the fondness for alcohol and women. When asked about it, Duchovny dodges. "We look alike," he says, "but he has better makeup. That's it."

**He's a fairly losing character, isn't he?**

"Yes, he loses. He loses a lot. But what I love about Hank, and the reason I was drawn to the part in the first place, is that he's a character who says what he thinks. He's a kind of truth-teller among all these people lying around him. That nature gives him a kind of power, and quite a few people even envy him. It's what we all want to do sometimes, but we can't, because we're afraid we'll get fired or dumped, or whatever it is."

**On the face of it, Hank has a good life. Why is he one of the most tormented characters on television?**

"I don't know why he's so miserable. Maybe because he's a writer and he isn't writing. He feels as if he was put on this earth to express something, and he isn't doing it. So yes, he has the things he wanted in terms of women and family, but something in the big picture isn't working."

**If you met Hank Moody in real life, what would you think of him?**

"I'm a father of two young children, so I don't see myself in a place where I'd run into him. Our worlds simply wouldn't connect. If I did come across him, I'd think he makes a lot of mistakes and still manages to be okay. He's exaggerated, almost grotesque, even though we try to play it realistically. I don't see Hank as just rude. I see him as the little guy fighting against the bullshit of life."

**You're considered a good father, in Hollywood terms. You've said more than once that it's important to you to spend quality time with your children.**

"Raising children isn't simple. There's no user manual. What I said earlier about Hank's truthfulness also applies to the way he raises his daughter. They're in a kind of blunt relationship, in terms of what they have and haven't hidden from each other. As a parent I know you can't judge Hank for his behavior. I think he's just groping in the dark like all of us. Parents mostly do the best they can, and so does Hank. I think what's lovely is that he clearly cares."

## A series in the dark

Duchovny may be strongly identified with Californication at the moment, but he does other things too. He tries to appear in a feature film every year (though few of them have become impressive box-office successes), and is currently working on three new films.

"The Californication schedule is really great for me," he says, "especially because I want to do other things, to write or direct or act in films. The series shoots only 12 weeks a year, so for me it's really a 13-week job. I prepare for the first episode a week in advance and then shoot 12 weeks straight. That lets me take time out to write, or to spend time with my family."

**Does it bother you that you're known as a television actor rather than a film actor?**

"No, because I go back and forth. I've done plenty of both. For me there's no difference, except that in film you can make a big pile of money all at once. But what I love about Californication is that I always wanted to express my comic side, and I wasn't really able to do that in film or on television until now. And still, I don't think I've ever felt like I'm really, really good. It's probably just not in my nature. I'll always aim for more than I've done. What I really want in the future is to write more, and maybe develop as a director too. I really want to do that."

**You're also the executive producer of Californication. Isn't that hard?**

"No, because I don't really deal with the practical side of production. I set the broad direction, and then I'm involved in casting and locations and all that. I leave the writing to Tom and his team. Usually I just try to make sure we're all comfortable on set and all in the same tone. That's our magic, the particular style we're in, the safe world we've created on the set of the series, which also translates to the screen."

**And we have to ask the question that will probably follow you to the end of your days: are there any plans to bring Mulder back to life?**

"There are no plans at the moment, but that doesn't mean we don't want to. I'm always happy to play that part and to recreate the world we built in The X-Files. The problem is that the films we made are no longer blockbusters. That's always a problem when you want to move forward with the studios, because at the end of the day the goal of a film is to make money. In any case, I wouldn't want Mulder to come back too old, or unfit for action. I think that would be sad. But I'm not ready to give up yet. I'm not ready to leave the sandbox."
