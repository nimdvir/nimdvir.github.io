**"I'm sick of being reminded in every interview that I'm from the Bronx. It's something that will always be with me, because that's where I come from and where I spent 20 years of my life, and still, I feel I've developed enormously since that period."** (Jennifer Lopez, on the magic years)

Nobody doubts that the distance between J.Lo and the block is as great as the one between the singer Sarit Hadad and the Hadera municipality. And yet, Lopez still maintains, almost desperately, the pose of the girl of the people, the one who may have trouble dragging herself around under the weight of the diamonds, but still keeps it real. Her new film, An Unfinished Life, provides a close-up meeting with that Lopez, the one who insists she's still the same simple girl, who didn't let the Hollywood pose turn her head. The one who at every opportunity she's given will prattle on with bottomless seriousness about how hard it is to be famous. "Those paparazzi, you know."

Unfortunately, it didn't take long to understand that Lopez, for all her good intentions and fine proportions, is not the regular girl she insists on selling. Lopez, as befits a girl who eats journalists like roasted pistachios, announced at the last minute that the interview was cancelled. Only after massive PR persuasion did she agree to consent to a press conference, while allotting an impossible amount of time for questions and answers. Needless to say, Lopez was also an hour and a half late for the hour set for the press conference. Fashionably late, of course. In the same spirit, at the film's premiere she marched straight into the hall, stubbornly refusing to play the red-carpet game. The reason: a stormy demonstration against her raging outside (explanations to follow). At the film's after-party too she carried on à la diva: she arrived at the party at New York's Mago club wrapped in her clan of an entourage and immediately skipped off into a private room. Not so much as one blown kiss was recorded. And finally, when the summit meeting finally took place, Lopez refused to answer a burning question, and even honored me with a look of fury that made me feel like a battered kitten.

But why get ahead of ourselves. Let's start with the premiere, held at the Directors Guild cinema in Manhattan. From the first moment it was clear things were going to be lively: outside the hall dozens of activists from ATEP, the well-known animal-welfare organization, had dug in, carrying signs attacking the actress personally. It turns out that Lopez, famous for her fondness for furs, has recently become the number one target of the activists, who make sure to honor every event she attends with a stormy demonstration. This time they came with dolls of slaughtered animals and signs bearing the relevant logo "J.Lo, you're responsible for 'Unfinished Lives.'" On the other side stood the groups of paparazzi, trying to guess how Lopez would react to the commotion this time. The answer wasn't long in coming: when the lady arrived, equipped with her husband Marc Anthony, it took her exactly one second to announce that she doesn't pose against a backdrop of corpses. "I'm not doing a red carpet here," she raged, and slipped quickly inside, turning a round little tush on the demonstrators and photographers waiting for her outside. The result: a mutual venting of aggression between the paparazzi and the demonstrators, the former yelling "your stupid demonstration cost us our livelihood," the latter screaming "you're helping to photograph the devil." Oh well.

After such a hot opening it was almost ironic to go inside and watch Lopez play a battered woman (and even do it well, to her credit), in contrast to the embarrassing turn she did in Enough, also a battered-women drama, which did her no favors. In An Unfinished Life, the new film by the respected Swedish director Lasse Hallström (Chocolat, The Cider House Rules), Lopez plays Jean, a hard-luck woman with a slew of secrets in her past. After taking one slap too many from her abusive boyfriend (Damian Lewis), she packs up her young daughter (Becca Gardner) and goes to settle in with her late husband's father (Robert Redford in cowboy pose). Daughter and mother find the old man caring for his injured friend (Morgan Freeman), and the foursome come to live together on the isolated ranch. As time ambles along everyone learns to deal with their fears, and finally they bond as a family. Lopez stands out in particular as a woman walking the line between strength and vulnerability, and manages to move as a mother who wants what's best for her daughter, and still manages to get it wrong time after time.

And back to reality, where Lopez is less a tired mother and more the New York version of Imelda Marcos. To the press conference held at the Essex House hotel on Central Park she arrives equipped with two enormous black bodyguards and an assistant whose main job was to open her water bottles. Mummified in a white suit and a hairdo borrowed from the politician Ora Namir, Lopez announces that time is short, the work is plentiful, so come on, let's talk. "I wanted something more dramatic, more character-driven," she opens her explanations about what brought her to the script. "Obviously the comedies I do get more attention, but this film is something I needed in order to move forward as an actress. There aren't many films like this in Hollywood, with so much emotion. At the end of the screening I had tears in my eyes, really."

**What was it like working with heavyweights like Robert Redford and Morgan Freeman?**

"When I started shooting the film I was at a good point in my career, so I could handle these people. Because I was confident, Robert and Morgan's presence didn't make me nervous. The one who went crazy was my mother: she grew up on Robert Redford, there was a period when she lived only for him. Since I was six I've been hearing his name. It's a kind of dream for her. When she came to visit the set she didn't come to me, she came to Robert. You could say that only now does my career have meaning for her."

**We're seeing you more and more in serious roles. Is that something deliberate?**

"Of course. I'm not the same person I was five years ago. I'm very happy to be going through a process of maturing, because I feel it improves me. Obviously it's fun to be 20, when everything's rosy, sweet and wonderful, but it's also an age at which it's hard to reach real emotional depth. Depth comes with experience, and I feel age only helps me improve as an actress."

**In the film you play a battered woman. Have you ever suffered abuse personally?**

"Apart from the media, nobody has abused me," she half-laughs, "but I can identify with the efforts to find inner strength. All the characters in the film have to face their bear, which symbolizes some kind of fear. I can completely understand that."

**Speaking of fear, what do you think about what's happening now in New Orleans?**

"My husband and I donated a lot of money to the Katrina victims. My thing now is to focus on what they really need there. For instance, right now our focus is on supporting medical treatment for children. We'll keep donating and helping, and we'll also raise more support through my husband's concerts."

**So what's actually going on with you now?**

"Now I'm calming down after a very stressful period. I'm on the road, accompanying my husband on his concert tour. It's a good thing for me right now, to be in the background and not up front. I made a decision that work will stop being the center of my life. So I still answer the phone, but let's say that these days you can no longer call me at 2:00 in the morning." "Last question," chirps the publicist. Despite the ticking clock, not one of the journalists of the world addressed last night's events and the question of why Jennifer insists on going head to head with the animal-rights groups. In a world where even the Uptown snobs enjoy wrapping themselves in synthetics, what exactly drives Lopez to stick fur on half the items in her wardrobe? With a trembling heart I stepped up to the microphone and tried my luck with Her Grace.

**"Yesterday there was a crazy**

**demonstration outside the hall. What do you have to say about it?"**

"No comment," said the lady coldly, suddenly on guard like a German prison warden.

**"Don't you at least feel like explaining what's going on there?"**

"I'm not interested in talking about it at all," she said, signaling her herd of people to extract her from there, and immediately.

Now, with nerves in her eyes and smoke coming out of her ears, Lopez suddenly looks a little different: from a smiling actress she turns in an instant into a kind of Don Corleone, that is, a tough businesswoman, armed with bodyguards and a combative pose. Why would she talk about furs? She is, after all, a businesswoman, and blood is very bad for business. And maybe that schizophrenia is in fact the secret of her charm: as long as up front she manages to remain the national sweetheart, it's probably no problem if behind the scenes she keeps functioning as a kind of tough mafiosa.
