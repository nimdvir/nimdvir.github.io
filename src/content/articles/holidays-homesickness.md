It took a long time before they let me sneak into the in-crowd's section, but at long last my turn has come to celebrate on the "Home Page" too. As the emissary to the diaspora, I hadn't exactly planned on barging in here on a holiday. Jennifer Lopez is releasing a film? That's good for 500 words. Jessica Alba decides to get off the broccoli? Cue the investigative report. And yet all this holiday fever brings with it an unexpected gust of sentimentality, which has made the past two weeks look a little like a Zehava Ben song. Granted, it's more fun over at Bush's tavern, but what can you do when the homesickness suddenly hits? The holiday atmosphere, which here comes down mostly to phone calls from Grandma back home, traditionally produces a general funk. Which means that as soon as the holidays arrive, the nostalgia pops right up: fantasies of wandering the streets on Yom Kippur, dreams of lounging with a fat stack of holiday newspapers, even fond memories of the family takeout dinners (we're Polish, you get used to it).

"What is there to miss?!" whined a friend on the phone from Israel, wrung out from a marathon of family mixers. "Believe me, next year I'm leaving all the nagging behind in Israel and coming to do laps around Macy's with you." What he doesn't understand is that the nagging is exactly what's missing. Like army food and Pnina Rosenblum, things that look like a problem up close take on the aroma of a hit from a distance: the grumbling around the table, Grandma's washed-out food, everyone's ability to see themselves as wasted talent, and above all the undisguised pleasure of digging around in each other's lives. Where would you find a party like that here, in a city whose local population of workaholics can't lift its head for even a second from the BlackBerry (a mobile phone that connects you, efficiently and suffocatingly, to your email inbox. Lucky you for not knowing).

If you happen to follow my column "America," the trashy love child I dispatch every week, you've probably already figured out that New York has no shortage of alternatives for the homesick. On the right, Meital Dohan is putting on a play; on the left, Eitan Schwartz is running between openings; and in the middle, a sea of Israeliness, from David Z's chain of shoe stores to Yitzhak Tshuva's Plaza Hotel. There's Tel Aviv-ness too, courtesy of a growing group of people who run their lives here as if they had just been uprooted from Dizengoff: directors, photographers, editors and assorted media types who came to conquer America and along the way congealed into a local media crowd. So they sit at Café Mogador on St. Marks Place, grumble about the situation back home, compete with one another nonstop, blow kisses at events and, above all, gossip like there's no day after tomorrow. Which proves that you can take the kids out of Tel Aviv, but you can't take Tel Aviv out of the kids. And still, it isn't really it.

Despite the community, and despite the fact that there are places to feel the holiday (I spent Rosh Hashanah with a gay couple in their sixties from New Jersey; stories some other time), the taste of home is still missing. As in every holiday season, this year too we arrive once more at the conclusion that the Israeli community in New York is a bit like Sucrazit: it may help sweeten the coffee, but it will always remain a kind of substitute. That's it, I've gotten mine off my chest, at least until next year.

Nimrod Dvir, New York
