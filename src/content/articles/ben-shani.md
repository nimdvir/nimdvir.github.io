Anyone who imagines Ben Shani, one of Israel's senior documentary journalists, glued to Channel 8 for most of the day is in for a surprise. "Yes," he smiles. "I watch Big Brother and MasterChef. I think I'm a consumer of television, and I like it when things are well made, when someone respects me, when they don't insult my intelligence. I appreciate the work on Big Brother. Turning out a flawlessly edited hour-and-a-half broadcast twice a week out of dozens of hours of raw footage is no simple task at all."

This coming week Uvda returns for season number 17 (Thursday, 9 p.m.), and Shani, now in his third year on the show, seems relaxed. No pager, no hysterical phone calls, no buzz of chasing scoops and deadlines. "I've always been like that," he attests. "I never sat on the pager. I was always a magazine man, working on a story in depth."

**With most journalists you expect to see pressure and nerves ahead of the first broadcast of the season.**

"With us the work is more horizontal. We work on stories all year round. The previous season of Uvda went off the air in May, and by June I'd already started working on stories for the current season. The fact that I have a few months to work on stories that are less tied to a broadcast date is a privilege. At Keshet (the network that airs Uvda; N.D.) they understand that for Uvda to keep its standard, the staff has to be active all the time, during the break too. An ordinary journalist doesn't have that privilege. That's what brought me to Uvda in the first place: the ability to work on those lengths of time. For years, when I worked on Channel 1's Yoman, I was programmed to a schedule that ended on Friday evening, which you work toward all week. Uvda let me get past the weekend.

"At first I had to change my thinking. Instead of 'what will be the main story this week,' to think about what will be the main story the week after. The bonus here is that after everyone leaves, you can get to the stories other people don't have the patience to reach."

## Ilana and I

Shani, 40, has been in the field for almost two decades. He started in local cable news, where he worked during his university studies (film and political science at Tel Aviv University). From there he moved to Channel 1's Yoman for a five-year stint, at the end of which he moved to Channel 10. In fact he was among the founders of the channel's news company and worked there until 2008, and over those years he made a series of documentaries that caused a stir, like Remedia's Children and Ralph. Then came the long-awaited call from Ilana Dayan, and Shani hurried to jump aboard the Uvda ship.

He has known Dayan for about a decade. "She came to present the Yoman edition as a stand-in," he says. "In 2002 talks started with Ilana about my joining the team, but Uvda was in an uncertain period then. At the same time Channel 10 approached me and wanted me full time. That was very tempting and I went there. And then, about three years ago, Ilana called again and asked me to come to Uvda. This time I agreed."

**What's it like working with her?**

"I always knew she was a phenomenon, but up close it's far more impressive. Usually, if I'm looking for someone to consult, she's the address. I call her before every interview to get advice and ideas from her on how to do it. She's an artist at it. The most impressive thing is to stand to the side and watch her work. It's not a given that someone will let you do that, and she let me watch her interviewing, with great pleasure. Ilana Dayan is Uvda. There's no Uvda without Ilana. She's involved in everything. Ilana defines the role herself: she presents, she's involved, and she sees every story. She accompanies and watches all the stories while they're being made. She prepares her own stories herself, at the level of being involved in the research, bringing in the subjects, talking to sources. She's also there at the shoots. However hard you work, she works harder, and still manages to keep the balance with family life better than you do. She's there at the events and in the life of her family and still does the job in the best possible way. She's very much a journalist."

Shani is a family man himself. He lives in Herzliya and is the father of three children: Uri, 10, Yonatan, 8, and Shiri, two. "My wife tells me I always have to be shooting stories, otherwise I go crazy," he laughs. "I always have to know what I'm working on. It's no accident that I started shooting stories in June, right after we went off the air."

**Did you always want to be a journalist?**

"I always wanted to be a journalist and do television, but my plan was a little different. I was sure I'd be a behind-the-scenes reporter and then, when I was experienced and seasoned, a reporter on Channel 1's Yoman. At the time that looked to me like the summit. It turned out my career actually started at Yoman."

**Like Ilana Dayan, are you a talent too?**

"I have no idea. When I studied film at university I heard the term for the first time. I didn't think I'd run into that word in journalism. It made sense to me with film stars, with people who sell a work or a film on the strength of their name. It's strange to me that the term has crept into journalism too. I suppose that if someone makes a story, people come to watch it because it's identified with him, meaning the journalist brings his own fingerprint. Something a little abnormal happened here: everything has become personal and branded."
