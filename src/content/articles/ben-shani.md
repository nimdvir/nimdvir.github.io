Anyone picturing Ben Shani, one of Israel's top documentary journalists, glued to Channel 8, the documentary channel, for most of the day is in for a surprise. "Yes," he smiles. "I watch Big Brother and MasterChef. I think of myself as a consumer of television, and I like it when things are well made, when somebody respects me, when my intelligence isn't insulted. I appreciate the work that goes into Big Brother. Turning dozens of hours of raw footage into an immaculately edited ninety-minute broadcast twice a week is no simple job at all."

This week Uvda, Channel 2's investigative newsmagazine, returns for its 17th season (Thursday, 9 p.m.), and Shani, in his third year on the show, looks relaxed. No pager, no frantic phone calls, no hum of the chase after scoops and deadlines. "I've always been like this," he says. "I was never on the pager. I was always a magazine guy, digging deep into one story."

**With most journalists you'd expect nerves and pressure before the season's first broadcast.**

"With us the work is spread out. We work on stories all year. The last season of Uvda went off the air in May, and by June I was already working on pieces for this one. Having a few months to work on stories that aren't chained to an air date is a privilege. At Keshet (the Channel 2 franchise that airs Uvda; N.D.) they understand that for Uvda to keep its standard, the newsroom has to stay active all the time, including the off-season. An ordinary journalist doesn't have that privilege. That's what brought me to Uvda in the first place: the chance to work on that kind of timescale. For years, when I was at Yoman, Channel 1's Friday-night newsmagazine, I was programmed to a schedule that ended Friday evening, and the whole week you work toward it. Uvda let me get past the weekend.

"At first I had to change the way I think. Instead of 'what's the big story this week,' it's what's the big story the week after. The bonus is that once everyone else has gone home, you can get to the stories nobody else has the patience for."

## Ilana and Me

Shani, 40, has been at it for nearly two decades. He started in local cable news, working there while he studied film and political science at Tel Aviv University. From there he moved to Channel 1's Yoman for a five-year stretch, and at the end of it to Channel 10. He was in fact one of the founding staff of the channel's news company and stayed until 2008, and in those years he made a string of documentaries that made noise, among them Remedia's Children (on Israel's 2003 baby-formula scandal) and Ralph. Then came the long-awaited call from Ilana Dayan, and Shani hurried aboard the Uvda ship.

He has known Dayan for about a decade. "She came in to anchor Yoman as a stand-in," he says. "In 2002 there was talk with Ilana about my joining the team, but Uvda was in an uncertain period then. At the same time Channel 10 approached me and wanted me full time. That was very tempting, and I went there. Then, about three years ago, Ilana called again and asked me to come to Uvda. This time I said yes."

**What's it like working with her?**

"I always knew she was a phenomenon, but up close it's far more impressive. Usually, when I need someone to think a thing through with, she's the one I go to. I call her before every interview for advice and ideas on how to do it. She's an artist at it. The most impressive thing is to stand off to the side and watch her work. It's not a given that anyone would let you do that, and she was happy to let me watch her interview. Ilana Dayan is Uvda. There's no Uvda without Ilana. She's involved in everything. Ilana defines the job herself: she anchors, she's hands-on, and she sees every story. She follows every piece while it's being made. She prepares her own stories herself, down to the research, bringing in the subjects, talking to sources. She's there at the shoots, too. However hard you work, she works harder, and still manages the balance with family life better than you do. She's at her family's events and in their lives and still does the job as well as it can be done. She is very much a journalist."

Shani is a family man himself. He lives in Herzliya with three kids: Uri, 10, Yonatan, 8, and Shiri, 2. "My wife says I always have to be shooting a story or I go crazy," he laughs. "I always need to know what I'm working on. It's no accident that I started shooting in June, the minute we went off the air."

**Did you always want to be a journalist?**

"I always wanted to be a journalist and do television, but my plan was a little different. I was sure I'd be a behind-the-scenes reporter and then, once I was seasoned and experienced, a reporter on Channel 1's Yoman. At the time that looked like the summit. As it turned out, my career started at Yoman."

**Like Ilana Dayan, are you a talent too?**

"No idea. I heard the term for the first time when I studied film at university. I didn't think I'd run into that word in journalism. It made sense to me for movie stars, people whose name sells a work or a film. It's strange to me that the term has seeped into journalism too. I suppose that if someone makes a story, people tune in because it's identified with him, meaning the journalist brings his own fingerprint. Something a little abnormal has happened here: everything has become personal and tied to a name."
