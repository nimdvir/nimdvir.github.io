The past year has been far from smiles for Julia Louis-Dreyfus, a woman who treats life with constant humor. "When I was diagnosed with breast cancer, I started crying," she says of the discovery of the malignant disease in her body in 2017.

It isn't easy to see a comedian in tears, but Dreyfus, having completed six rounds of chemotherapy and surgery, already sounds and looks recovered. "The treatments and the surgery were very hard. I felt sick to the depths of my soul, but I always knew I was going to come out of it, that I had to come out.

"Obviously cancer makes you think. We're not the same when we see the end in front of our eyes. I saw death. You could say that before the illness I was a lot more lighthearted, but the illness made me understand that lightness isn't something I can afford. It's not that I'm heavy, I'm just a lot more awake and goal-oriented."

Dreyfus, who shared her journey with her fans on social media, said this week in an interview with "Good Morning America": "I think I reached a lot of people. I raised a lot of money for women who have had a mastectomy and reconstruction. Everyone's support helped me get through the chemotherapy. And now I'm completely back."

Next week she returns to the screen in the seventh and final season of the political comedy she stars in, "Veep" (to air on yes, HOT and Cellcom TV from April 1). "I didn't doubt for a second that this season would make it to the screen," she says. "If anything, the cancer gave me motivation. They say humor is the best medicine, and that's not a cliché. I needed the laughter in my life, that purpose."

* * *

Since she first appeared as Elaine Benes in "Seinfeld," Dreyfus (58) has become one of the most acclaimed actresses in history, with a record-breaking number of Emmy awards (nine for "Seinfeld," six of them for her acting) and other honors. After a few seasons on the series "The New Adventures of Old Christine," she stepped into the shoes of the seasoned politician Selina Meyer in the hit "Veep." In the final season her character once again runs for the presidency of the United States, surrounded by her team of ne'er-do-wells and armed with a cynical attitude to life.

The season has Meyer tossing out a slew of inappropriate jokes about the disabled, about victims of shooting attacks, about people from the lower classes and about anyone who crosses her retina. All she cares about is the White House.

"You could say I've made a career out of playing pretty terrible characters," Dreyfus laughs when we meet this week at a New York hotel. "But even when Meyer is really terrible, I don't see it that way. She's very determined and very, very frustrated. There's something interesting in frustration. Behind it there's a lot of sadness and ambition. It's a lot deeper than just being 'terrible.' So she takes her frustration out on everyone around her.

"It kills me how she talks to people. For instance, one of her lines dressing down an employee who messed up: 'It's like using a croissant as a dildo: it doesn't do the job and it leaves a mess!'" She cracks up.

**You talk about her as if she were a real person.**

"She exists. You see her everywhere around us. She's a combination of a lot of women and men in politics and in other industries. You could say it's also a series about Hollywood, not just about Washington. A series about selfish people trying to advance themselves in every possible way."

**How much are you like Selina?**

"Tons, loads," she laughs. "Mostly I can identify with her. I understand her frustration: being a woman who is busy all day with how to sell her brand. I do the same thing. There's a lot of similarity between surviving in politics and surviving in showbiz. Being a middle-aged woman trying to stay relevant and influential: I definitely understand the feeling. When the people across from you think you've run your course and should go home, or when they don't take you seriously, it's infuriating."

**At your standing, you still feel that way?**

"Yes. I've always felt I had to fight. It was hard for me to be granted the ability to produce my own material. I fought very hard to get creative control. There was a lot of pressure against me, but in the end I won."

**What's the trick?**

"I can honestly say I have tons of experience in entertainment. I'm full of confidence that I have a lot to add."

* * *

Dreyfus is the show's undisputed boss. The whole crew is at her feet: paying her endless compliments, holding its breath when she crosses the set. And still, she seems to work harder than anyone.

At the hotel where the interview takes place she has been at it since seven in the morning, without a break. Hopping between interviews with media from around the world, huddling with the show's producers, closing loose ends in the editing of the coming episodes.

Dreyfus takes off her high heels and stays barefoot. She sits down next to the producer in the hotel corridor, watches clips on the laptop screen, and hands out editing instructions. "I'm hearing 'ahem' too many times," she remarks, and suggests shortening a scene. From there she carries on with the round of interviews, and there isn't a drop of tiredness in her.

"A good leader is one who manages to set an example," Tony Hale, who plays her right-hand man on the show, says of her. "Julia is like that. She's never off. She never has a bad day. And even if she does, you'll never know. She comes to work."

Dreyfus is modest about it: "I think we're all trying to do the best we can. It's absolutely not just my show. Everyone works very hard."

**How does your role as a producer play out?**

"It's a role that never ends. I'm involved in everything. At least I've changed. I used to watch every scene shot on a given day, like a person possessed, but I've learned to let go. When I watched myself I'd fill up with self-criticism, with loathing and shame. Today I'm no longer that hard on myself."

**You seem to work harder than anyone.**

"It's important to me, on a personal level. After the show's original creator (Armando Iannucci; N.D.) left after the fourth season, I felt someone had to take the reins. I had to take command. I'm glad I did it, that I pushed the HBO network to continue. I'm very proud of that.

"I very much appreciate all the people who work with me, and it's important to me to serve as a kind of example or inspiration for them. This product is the result of the joint work of 200 people, and to me that's the magic of television. On the face of it, it seems impossible to get 200 people to do anything. And here we are, managing to put out episodes."

**Why are you ending?**

"It was a decision by me and the producers. The network was willing to let us continue, but I felt the show had run its course. We're ending at the point where I think we need to end."

**Is there a connection to reality itself having become satirical to a degree that outdoes the scripts?**

"Given the current climate, it's a lot more challenging to try to push comic boundaries. On the other hand, from the start we have operated on the show without needing the characters' political identity. None of them is defined as being on a particular side of the political divide, and we live in a confining universe that helps us move the story forward, certainly now. In my opinion, that's the key to why our show ran for so many seasons. As far as we're concerned, everyone is invited to the 'party' we throw on screen every episode.

"There's no doubt that reality today is a lot tougher. When we started it was Barack Obama's administration, and look what's happening in the White House today. All sorts of things we joked about on the show have turned into unfunny jokes in reality. The current climate definitely affects the feeling. Maybe it isn't funny anymore. Donald Trump isn't funny in my eyes. I mean, he could have been funny if he didn't have the power he has."

**I gather you're not a big fan of the current president.**

"To put it mildly. It shows in everything he does."

**Is there nothing in common between you, as a president on screen, and him?**

"The only thing is that we're both pretend presidents. He's playing a president too. He's a make-believe president, a pretense."

**Although Trump's spirit hovers above it, there's no mention of him in the current season.**

"That's deliberate. From the start the idea was not to represent reality in absolute terms, but to be a satire of politics in general. This is actually the first season in which Trump was part of our lives. He was elected in 2016, after the six previous seasons had already been written. This season was written in 2017, and it was very hard to ignore what Trump is doing. Everywhere in the world people ask me about him, he's become the face of America. That's very sad in my eyes. I didn't want to take part in it."

**Do you find yourself laughing at the news, which, as noted, outdoes every line in the script?**

"Yes, there are times when that's exactly what I think to myself. Suddenly I feel it's so extreme, even for our show. And still, it's important for me to note that I play a woman who served as president of the United States, and that is already a reality we don't know, a boundary that hasn't yet been breached.

"I do feel the groundwork is there, that the ground has been prepared. Without going into detail, I think that's a situation that has already been taken into account in the new season's episodes. In many cases the show touches on all sorts of real scenarios, whether intentionally or not, and that has been its character for the whole period we've been on the air."

**Do you find similarities between actors and politicians?**

"Absolutely, you're a kind of brand that relies on relevance, selling an image to the public. You're in a never-ending struggle to stay in the game. Every member of Congress who gets elected wants to keep his seat, and his head is already in the next campaign. An actor who gets a job also starts thinking about the next project so as not to end up unemployed.

"In both cases you depend on the current role, which in effect dictates which role you'll move on to. Add gender to the equation: I'm a woman who has been working in the industry for four decades, and the older I get, the harder it becomes to stay in the picture. That's true in politics too. Maybe in Washington it actually adds power to women, as holders of experience and connections from their advanced years, but that's only true for very particular women in specific positions of power."

**Why not, actually, bring the reality outside into the show?**

"Because it's political satire and not a parodic commentary on the existing situation. I love parody, certainly everything to do with the wonderful work they do on 'Saturday Night Live,' but that's not our show.

"I grew up and learned a great deal in my three years on 'Saturday Night Live,' in the early '80s. The competition was lethal, it was not a friendly environment for women, to put it mildly. It was a wild, drug-soaked scene I didn't connect with, but it helped me sharpen what I did want to do as an actress. In my last year there I met Larry David, and then I found a partner for my misery.

"But on 'Veep' you can't compete with reality, and in fact we don't mention any president who came after Ronald Reagan. We have a fictional history. It proved itself in hindsight, because the US is so divided, and still everyone can feel that their political position is supposedly represented.

"We have advisers who worked in the Obama administration and in the Bush administration before it. I get responses from both political extremes, each one delighted at how much we make fun of the other side. I'm amused every time it happens, because it's proof that we're doing a good job."

**Do you feel you represent American politics because of the show?**

"When I travel in foreign countries people always ask my opinion of Trump. Before they even recognize me as Julia the actress, the moment they hear me speaking English with an unmistakable American accent, the first thing they want to know is what I think of Trump. Apparently that's what most fascinates people outside the US. Of course they also recognize me from 'Seinfeld' or from other characters I've played over the years. I was on a ski holiday in the Alps in Austria, and maybe because of the snowy atmosphere there was someone there who recognized me from 'National Lampoon's Christmas Vacation' (a Christmas film from 1989, in which she acted alongside Chevy Chase; N.D.). That was unexpected."

* * *

She wasn't originally supposed to be in "Seinfeld." The idea was to make an all-male show, but the NBC network insisted on adding a female character, and Elaine felt like an unwanted addition.

"It took me time to find my place," she says of her days on the legendary series. "The truth is that for years I felt they weren't giving me jokes with 'meat.' I had jokes, but they weren't as strong as the men's, to my taste." And yes, she admits that part of the time she felt she was in a boys' club. "Many times I heard Larry David and Jerry Seinfeld say they write Elaine as if she were a guy."

In shaping the character she set herself the goal of being a voice for women in comedy. Dreyfus is known as a mentor to successful comedians of the new generation, such as Amy Schumer or Abbi Jacobson of "Broad City." Jacobson even said of her in an interview: "The characters we created on 'Broad City' are a direct result of watching Julia and understanding that you're allowed to 'be like that' as a woman."

Dreyfus is modest about it: "I don't think I 'held the door' for anyone. I don't even understand what that means. Every woman earns her place honestly. I do see progress in the entertainment industry. There are a lot more roles for women in comedy than 20 or 30 years ago. There are roles that aren't just being 'the wife of' or the hot girlfriend. Most of the characters I've played were single. I pretty much loathe 'the adoring girlfriend' roles."

**Jerry Seinfeld said in the past that political correctness is nonsense and "anti-comedy." Do you agree?**

"Absolutely not. I'm in favor of political correctness. You could say I'm even a little suspicious of people who oppose it. I think what they're actually saying is that it's fine to tell racist jokes or jokes about violence against women. That's really not fine."

Well-founded gossip has stuck Louis-Dreyfus with the image of an heiress, a scion of a billionaire family. Her father, William, ran the Louis Dreyfus Group between 1996 and 2006, an investment company with holdings in real estate, gas, communications and more.

"The media reports about my father's wealth are greatly exaggerated," she says defensively. "They like to write that he's a billionaire, and about me they often write that I'm a billion-dollar heiress. That's really not the case. Far from it." Then again, when her father passed away a decade ago he may not have left billions behind, but there's no arguing with the fact that this is a rich-plus family.

The art collection alone at the father's country house in upstate New York was valued after his death at 50 million dollars. The collection, together with other assets, was transferred to an educational foundation in his name that helps underprivileged children. Louis-Dreyfus is one of the foundation's directors. According to reports, her personal fortune currently sits in the region of 200 million dollars.

Last summer her sister, Emma, passed away at the age of 44. The cause of death was defined as a "stroke," and before long reports began surfacing that quantities of cocaine and alcohol had been found in the sister's blood. The gossip papers hurried to declare that the death was caused by an overdose. The fact that Louis-Dreyfus didn't respond publicly and refused to be interviewed on the subject only fanned the flames, and tabloid covers claimed the sisters had been at odds and weren't speaking to each other.

In an interview she gave recently to the "New Yorker" magazine, Louis-Dreyfus agreed to break her silence: "I kept quiet out of respect for my sister. It was a very, very hard time for all of us, shocking even. I didn't feel it was right to talk about it with the media," she explained.

**What do you think of Israel?**

"I've never been there, but I'd be very happy to come visit. My grandfather is Jewish, but I don't identify as Jewish. It's part of my heritage, of the mosaic that makes up my identity, but there are other parts."

* * *

Louis-Dreyfus insists she's less of a workaholic than Selina, but not much less. She recently finished shooting a feature film with Will Ferrell, and now she's looking for the next challenge.

"There's no television on the table right now, but I'm open to offers," she answers with a smile. "I won't say 'no' to a good script."

**Would you do political comedy again?**

"I don't think so. I learned a lot about politics from this show. All in all, I understood that politicians are just people. That's all. On one hand that's comforting, and on the other it's terrifying, considering they're the ones who have to run the show. It's like visiting a sausage factory: you don't really want to see how they're made."
