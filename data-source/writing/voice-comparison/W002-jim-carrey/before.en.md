*English translation of the Hebrew original by Nimrod Dvir, published in Ynet on January 18, 2006. [Read the original in Hebrew](https://www.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-3202355,00.html).*

**Nimrod Dvir, New York**

They used to tell us that crime doesn't pay. Then again, they also used to tell us that Jim Carrey was a serious madman. Starting this week, both of those notions are about to change: meet Fun with Dick and Jane, a fine social satire disguised as a holiday movie.

*Jim Carrey Téa Leoni*

From a distance, Dick and Jane looks like yet another family comedy, the kind in which Jim Carrey mostly pulls faces: the story is about Dick (Jim Carrey) and Jane (Téa Leoni), a suburban couple who suffer a surprise financial crash. In their distress the two are forced to go rob stores, and set out on a Robin Hood crusade to get their self-respect back. Sounds light, but up close you discover that this is nothing less than an advertisement for anarchy: the film gleefully proclaims the advantages of crime, and along the way slips in poisoned arrows about Botox, foreign workers, George Bush and other musty subjects in today's America.

Like the film, Carrey, 43, surprises up close. Sitting beside Leoni at New York's Waldorf Astoria, the comic genius chooses to forgo the familiar rubber face and goes instead for spreading social messages: "The film deals with a very serious subject in a funny way," he opens. "Think about the story: two people slide down to the poverty line, get dragged into the world of crime. In an instant this could have turned into a tearjerker."

**So what were you actually trying to say?**

Carrey: "The situation today is that the American corporate economy is a rotten apple, and we all keep nibbling at it as long as we don't get sick. There's an exposed nerve in our country: the worsening class gaps and the worship of money are dumbing us down more than ever. A CEO used to earn 40 times what the simple worker earned; today he earns 400 times. It's just vulgar! Takes me back to the days of the Roman Empire."

**Hold on, you also count your salary in the millions.**

"Money is great, but it's a symptom, not the goal. I never worked for the paycheck. I knew that if the day came when I got paid 10 million dollars, it wouldn't mean I had money, it would mean I had the power to create what I really want."

Meet Jim Carrey, 2005 model: with power, with vision, and above all, after the Dumb and Dumber era. Don't expect jokes from him, at least not off screen. Today's Carrey is a beginner philosopher, a poet of clichés, though his observations are a good deal more entertaining: "I'm a clarity freak now," he answers when asked what gives him the strength to carry on, "and my energy source is meditation. I've been stoned, I've been wasted, I've been everything, and now I just want to be so clear that I'll turn into a ball of light and fly off the planet."

**Without ruining the ending, the couple isn't really punished for all the robberies it commits. Is that what happens when you get clear, you realize crime is great?**

"It's not that the film says 'OK, you're having a hard time financially, pick up a gun and go rob a bank.' I see it more as a statement that in today's situation, to survive you need to know how to bend the rules."

**Dick and Jane is based on a 1977 film, originally with Jane Fonda. Why do a cover version right now, of all times?**

"It's true there's a remake disease right now, but this isn't The Godfather, which people automatically know. We thought it was a film that suits the 21st century, with everything going on with Enron (a large American company that crashed in a way similar to the company shown in the film; N.D.) and with our president, on whom Alec Baldwin, my boss in the film, based his character. It was important to me to make a film that also has a message, that shows our economy in a slightly different light."

**Do you remember times when you were poor yourself?**

"Of course! I once worked as a janitor and a security guard at a steel plant. I was 16, and my father had just become unemployed. I went to school by day, worked at night and, mostly, I was an angry guy all the time. I was angry at what the universe had done to me and my father. Ever since, I clean my own toilets, I understand the janitors' suffering."

A lot of water has gone over Niagara since the janitor days. Carrey, who burst into consciousness as a gifted comedian in films like Ace Ventura and The Mask, began developing himself in parallel as a serious actor too. In The Truman Show he moved audiences as a man who falls victim to the world of reality TV; in Man on the Moon he stepped into the shoes of the legendary comedian Andy Kaufman, and in the beautiful Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind he dissects the connection between love and memory. Now, as noted, he's going for the middle ground: on one side he delivers a comedy that's a riot, and on the other, poverty isn't exactly a Brothers Grimm fairy tale.

**You've come a long way since your days as Ace Ventura.**

"I'm evolving and proud to be revealing different sides in varied projects. I'm very proud of Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind, because I think we changed something in the language of cinema with it. In the same way, I'm also proud of Dumb and Dumber, which has its own place too. I feel I'm changing something in my work, certainly."

**Apart from the message, what did you enjoy most about shooting the film?**

"I loved all the creative ways we find to disguise ourselves during a robbery. Like, there's a bit in the film where we put these voice distorters on so no one will recognize us. It was just hilarious: everything you say sounds funny! That scene is entirely improvised, and afterward we sat up until 4:00 in the morning laughing with those synthesizers. Another funny bit was when we dressed up as Sonny and Cher. Originally Téa was supposed to be Cher and I'd be Sonny, but when the costumes arrived we said 'why don't we actually switch?' Téa fit Sonny's clothes perfectly. I, on the other hand, was a very ugly Cher."

**For most of the film you're glued to Téa. Did you work a lot on the couple's chemistry before the shoot?**

"The truth is the first time I met Téa was seven hours before filming. For me, showing up to a shoot without rehearsals is like showing up to the first day of school without pants. Luckily, we had a spark from the first moment. Originally, the romantic bit between the characters wasn't supposed to be emphasized at all. Dick and Jane's love came straight from us. Téa goes along with my nonsense. My jokes just bounce off her, and vice versa. She's a comically flexible girl, very cooperative."

**In the film, part of what drives the characters is who has the nicer car, the more prestigious job and so on. Do you feel the pressure of "who's got the bigger one" too?**

"Nothing to be done, the neighbor's grass always looks greener. We have a bit in the film where my character steals grass, literally, from the neighbors. The funny thing is that this week I saw a news report about a couple of dentists who stole their neighbors' lawn. Apparently it's less far-fetched than we thought."

**A whole interview has gone by and not a single word about your private life. Not your cup of tea, letting the media into the house?**

"I'm very lucky that I never turned into one of those tabloid things. Yes, there are photographers who follow me, but luckily I was never dragged into that mud of gossip. Not that there's anything wrong with exposure, I love exposing myself (laughs), but my personal life is a private matter. There isn't too much pressure on me to share: there's so much media today that people are simply desperate to be noticed and are willing to do anything. With me, in any case, love is not for sale. Besides, I don't go out at all."

**Why is that?**

"People say to me 'let's go to a club,' and I say 'let's sit at home and expand our consciousness.' I sound like a psychopath, right? But my life is like an experiment. I'm always looking for answers: reading about new things, trying new things in food, trying new people, and I'm not afraid to experiment with films either. Today, my main form of expression is films. I used to paint, and now I make cinema: drama, comedy, sculpture, painting, it's all the same thing. For me, the taste of life isn't making millions, or starring in a sex scandal that feeds the papers. For me, the peak of ambition is to be a creative human being."
