*Bringing together the ten most successful and capricious drag queens in the United States is no small matter. Not to mention getting them to behave nicely, like big girls, while they are being filmed for a new reality series. Our correspondent gawked at them behind the scenes of "The Drag Queens" and came back with colorful conclusions*

*English translation of the Hebrew original by Nimrod Dvir, published in nrg Maariv on June 28, 2010. [Read the original in Hebrew](https://www.makorrishon.co.il/nrg/online/47/ART2/125/775.html).*

Let's open with old news: America is crazy about reality shows. Hair salons, tanning salons, children, dog breeders, rich people in Los Angeles, hipsters in New York, dysfunctional families, families with many children, all these and more are documented there by cameras that follow them day and night, then edited into heated episodes, for the benefit of the ratings.

Joining this endless harvest now is the show "The Drag Queens of New York," a new reality series that follows the ten most prominent drag queens in Manhattan in their daily lives. I followed this series for two days of filming. Apart from learning something about the queens (for example, that they are all looking for love), I also discovered a few secrets about the production process of American reality, for example that scenes are shot there again and again, that locations are chosen carefully and that the dramatic situations are well organized and well timed. This, of course, is nothing a veteran viewer of "The Hills" or "Keeping Up with the Kardashians" doesn't already know. And still.

At the time of filming the show had no broadcaster, and it was produced to be sold to one of the channels. Only last month was the die cast: it was reported that the series' episodes were sold to Here!, an American gay cable channel, which will also try to distribute them around the world as they air. In addition, shortened versions of the episodes and behind-the-scenes clips will be shown on the leading gay website Gay.com. Something of a coup.

"The Drag Queens" belongs to the genre of the hit brand "The Real Housewives," a sort of reality version of the hit series "Desperate Housewives." The concept is based on a group of housewives, if you can call them that, who live in oversized houses and meddle in one another's lives. In this case the veteran format gets a gay twist: the stars of "The Drag Queens" are real drag queens who live in New York, mingle, stir the pot and so on, exactly like the "Housewives," except that under the makeup, the glitter and the heels are men.

Four people are responsible for the show: John Rutherford (owner of Colt Studios, which produces, among other things, gay porn films), Larry Kennar (a producer and director who previously produced the hit lesbian drama "The L Word"), Jack Mackenroth (known to the public also from his participation in the fourth season of "Project Runway") and Lady Bunny (one of the best-known drag queens in America).

Lady Bunny (John Ingle, originally) has been active for several decades in the United States and among other things organizes the Wigstock festival, New York's annual drag festival. She gathered her friends and assembled a dream team that includes the classy Hedda Lettuce, who tries to wear only green and has won the title "Drag of the Year" from the American gay magazine "HX" six times; Sherry Vine, the queen of downtown Manhattan, who also appears in quite a few films; Bianca Del Rio, the cabaret artist with the sharpest tongue in the West; Peppermint, a Black sex bomb; Mimi Imfurst, a well-known trash artist; Acid Betty, a glam-rock drag queen with insane makeup; and other names more and less familiar.

In any case this is a highly prestigious bunch in New York terms. Lady Bunny we've already covered. Hedda Lettuce hosts huge events across America and has even appeared on various television shows (for example, contestants on "Project Runway" had to design a dress for her). Sherry Vine and Peppermint are considered icons of the community in the United States, and their YouTube videos, in which they parody Lady Gaga songs, have drawn millions of views. Acid Betty is considered a makeup guru. "These are very big stars in the world they operate in," laughs producer Rutherford.

**How did you manage to persuade them to join the production?**

"First of all, the project aroused interest in the community. There isn't a person we told about the idea of a reality show about drag queens who didn't think it was going to be a hit. Besides, the people responsible for the project are very well known in the American gay community. Lady Bunny, Jack Mackenroth, Larry Kennar and yours truly have been working for years in the entertainment industry and in the gay community. When we said we were the ones behind this project, people knew they could trust us."

## Their Majesties

Noon, midweek, the Aspen restaurant in Manhattan's Chelsea neighborhood. Today they are shooting a scene here with all the queens, in which they hold a sort of talent night at the restaurant. Each one takes the stage in turn and does a short number for the guests' enjoyment. Along the way, as befits reality TV, they also fight with one another, shove one another off the stage and all that fun. I was invited here as a guest and spent most of the day sipping martinis and gawking at the stage. Look for my looks of astonishment when the episode airs.

After ten minutes of filming it is already possible to point to two problems with the "reality" of this reality show: 1. The scene was shot at midday (though it will presumably be presented on screen as a scene that took place in the evening), in a restaurant closed specially for the occasion, and the audience consisted entirely of friends of the production.

2. Never mind that the queens' show was planned, but it looked as if even the internal fights among them, the very heart of reality TV, had done a round or two in the rehearsal room. And if a line wasn't caught nicely on camera, everything stopped and they went again. True, everyone knows this is how the business in question works, but it's a little jarring to see it actually happen. "You can't say we're not a reality show," Rutherford protests firmly when I pull out the accusation. "True, we closed the restaurant for the filming, but everything that happens is real."

**Not exactly. With my own eyes I saw you reshoot, for example, a mini-fight between the queens Bianca Del Rio and Lady Bunny.**

"True, originally there were some exchanges between them that weren't filmed well and we asked them to repeat the scene, but everything that will be seen on screen is real. The girls didn't plan what to say. True, there are constraints, but that's the business in television. At the end of the day we want the product to look good, so sometimes you have to be a little flexible. You can't say the show is scripted. It's all real."

**And what about the fact that the restaurant was closed for the occasion at noon?**

"Imagine we were shooting in a 'real' restaurant at rush hour," says Jack Mackenroth. "You know what would happen? They wouldn't let us work. There are a lot of people involved in this production and we can't waste time. So yes, we closed a restaurant with our own guests, but the audience, including you for that matter, didn't know what it was coming to or what was going to happen. All the reactions here are real. Everyone involved in the production has experience with reality shows. That's how it is on every show."

**You yourself took part in a reality show, "Project Runway," as a contestant. Was the situation similar there too?**

"Of course. I got to see how that show works from the inside, because I'm also a fashion designer. 'Project Runway' isn't scripted, exactly like our show isn't scripted, but on 'Project' too they stopped filming, re-recorded scenes and created situations to stir up interest. You'd have to be naive to think that doesn't happen. What matters is that the core is real."

While Kennar directs the cameras (in this particular scene they used three), Hedda Lettuce sits off to the side, her expression glum. Here too she has come in her standard outfit: a green dress, a greenish wig and a sour attitude to life. "The girls hate me," she says. "Look at them, not one of them wants to be my friend."

**So why are you here?**

"You think I'd let these bitches steal my spotlight? Of course I'll come. Just kidding, they don't really hate me. They're jealous of me."

**But everything here is fake, isn't it?**

"Honey, look at me: what is fake? If you're asking seriously, then no, nobody told us what to say or what to do. I suppose in that respect the show is real. It is entertainment, like everything we do. Don't take everything so seriously! You're probably going to write now that I'm feuding with the other girls."

**You're not? You just said you were.**

"A little humor, darling. Nothing is serious in the world of television, reality shows or drag queens. Here you've got all three ingredients, so what exactly do you expect?"

**Do you think the show will succeed?**

"Of course. No reason it shouldn't. We're far more interesting than the women of 'The Real Housewives' or the girls of 'The Hills.' Look around you. It's a hit waiting to happen. But I don't think about it. I always said 'don't think about it, do it,' so here too I didn't think too much. They called, so I came. Wait, don't write that down, okay? I don't want people thinking I have free time."

Compared with Hedda Lettuce's diva attitude, Lady Bunny, THE drag queen of New York, is much softer. "Sweetie, why aren't you being filmed?" she asks when she spots me sipping a cup of coffee off to the side.

**I'm on a break, and I don't want to steal the focus from the girls.**

"Believe me, with the amount of egos here it would be very hard to steal the focus from anyone."

**I noticed that earlier you fought with Bianca Del Rio.**

"Don't believe everything you see, sugar. We're very good friends. That's just our way, the queens of New York. We're constantly tearing each other down. It's part of the charm. Underneath it all there's a ton of love. We don't really hate. It's part of the game."

## We went looking for love

The next day we meet at the luxurious Ace Hotel in the heart of New York. This hotel, for those who don't know, is a trendy, ultra-designed hotel. The rooms have an electric guitar, just for show. They are shooting the testimonials here now, the queens' personal confessions, with a little gimmick: the queens talk about life while putting on their makeup, each in her own room, turning from man into woman. I sit in one of the rooms with Acid Betty, a deranged drag queen whose quantities of makeup would make even Pnina Rosenblum, Israel's cosmetics queen, go pale.

The two of us eavesdropped on Dallas DuBois, another drag queen who sat in the bathroom smearing makeup on her face while being documented. Honestly? It was moving. DuBois told the film crew about her yearning for love, about what a relationship means to her ("to know there's someone who cares about me, no matter what") and about how she came to the field (she was a music student and was looking for a way to perform). Then she told about her last boyfriend, whom she loved very much, until he discovered she was starting to make moves in drag and dumped her.

Acid Betty is a music student too. "I started five years ago, when I'd just arrived in the city," she says, straight-faced. "I was a student and I'm still a student. Here, in any case, it's a nice living. We're like a family. All the queens know each other, we perform together, meet up after work too, go out, hang out. You know, in a city like this it's very important to have support."

**Dallas talks about looking for love. Is that feeling shared by all of you?**

"I won't speak for the others, but there's a yearning for a relationship, exactly like anyone else. There are quite a few people who are put off by our appearance or by our particular sense of humor, so it isn't simple to find a relationship."

It isn't only relationships that trouble the girls. Hedda Lettuce, who at the filming was a bubbling spring of madness, arrives at the hotel ahead of a fateful scene in which she prepares for a meeting with her father. "This is going to be intense," she smiles. "The whole drag thing is very hard for him. It's the first time we're being exposed in front of the camera."

**So why are you doing it?**

"Because I said that if I'm doing this, then it's all the way. I agreed to be exposed both as a woman and as a man, and that's an important step for me. It's going to be an interesting show, no doubt."

The question, of course, is whether viewers will think so too. From this month eight episodes of the show will air on Here!, with a second season in the works. "We're optimistic," smiles Rutherford. "It's a winning team in a glittering package. What could go wrong?"
