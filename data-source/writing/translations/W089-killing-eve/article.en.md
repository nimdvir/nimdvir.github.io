*What made television critics declare Killing Eve a perfect show? How did Jodie Comer become a fashion icon playing a psychopathic assassin? And will the success land Sandra Oh the role of James Bond? With the crime drama back for a second season, our correspondent met the stars and came home with a scolding from Sandra*

*English translation of the Hebrew original by Nimrod Dvir, published in Israel Hayom on April 11, 2019. [Read the original in Hebrew](https://www.israelhayom.co.il/magazine/shishabat/article/9324631).*

![Photo: PR // Jodie Comer and Sandra Oh. "In the second season we explore this impossible romance between the two of them more deeply"](https://www.israelhayom.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/22/15549914301345_b-960x640.jpg)

*Photo: PR // Jodie Comer and Sandra Oh. "In the second season we explore this impossible romance between the two of them more deeply"*

BuzzFeed crowned it "the most perfect show for our times." Vanity Fair picked it as the most significant fashion event on television. The Daily Mail reported that on Halloween a huge number of revelers dressed up as characters from the show, making it the year's leading costume. And if all that isn't enough, it broke a record in the history of the British BAFTA awards when it recently received nominations for 14 prizes, in nearly every major category.

Killing Eve, that's the name, and if you don't know it, you are not a party to "one of the great cultural events of the year," as the press called it. The BBC America thriller-comedy-action-passion series appeared in 90 percent of the rankings of the world's 127 leading television critics, usually in the top three, overtaking celebrated shows like Atlanta and The Americans.

The first season premiered in the United States exactly a year ago. At its center is Eve Polastri (Sandra Oh, Dr. Cristina Yang from Grey's Anatomy), an American living in London with her wimp of a husband and working at the British intelligence agency MI6. Her routine breaks when she manages to squeeze onto a team investigating the activity of a mysterious hired assassin called Villanelle, played by Jodie Comer, one of last year's great discoveries.

Over the course of the season it emerges that Villanelle works for a secret organization called The Twelve, which sends her to carry out killings across Europe. Eve sets out after her, and between the two women a bizarre, codependent relationship develops, with the lines between love and hate blurring fast.

It is an adaptation of the British book series by the novelist Luke Jennings, but don't expect the books to help you find out how it ends. From the start it was clear that the television script allowed itself to go in completely different directions, chiefly with the choice to put Eve's character at the center and focus on the relationship between the two. In the first book of the series, Codename Villanelle, Eve is not mentioned at all.

* * *

Like the characters they play, Oh and Comer make a point of displaying symbiosis and polarity in real life. It shows in the look: for the interview marking the second season (which premiered this week on HOT and NEXT TV) they arrive together, dressed in suits of almost identical cut, except that Comer's is entirely black and Oh's is snow white. Could it be any clearer? And although the symbolism is hard to ignore (black and white, angel and devil), they insist emphatically that they did not coordinate their outfits.

"From the moment we met, there was a strange, strong chemistry between us," says Oh, and Comer nods. "I think we listen very closely to each other, on a very deep level. Each of us moves according to the other's rhythm. That makes us very good partners.

"It's not that we sit up at night texting each other about what to wear and how to behave. It just happens. We have a chemistry based on listening and mutual attention. The dynamic between the two of us is what makes this relationship so interesting. It's the force that drives the show."

"On the other hand, it's the most screwed-up relationship on television right now!" Comer laughs. "There's some unhealthy force pushing them toward each other. They're fascinated by each other, not necessarily for the same reasons. Eve is drawn to Villanelle's freedom and even to her violence, as you discover as the show develops.

"For Villanelle the attraction is based on respect. She admires Eve's intelligence and what she has achieved in her career. On the other hand, she wants her life, the 'normality' she has built for herself.

"And there's also a sexual component, desire. In the second season we explore this impossible romance between the two of them more deeply, how far it can go, and how long it can last."

**So it's really a show about a love story between two opposite women who discover they're more alike than they thought?**

"I hate that question!" Oh rules, "and I'll tell you why. I feel that people are constantly trying to put us in boxes, to make us explain exactly what the show is about and what the poet meant. But it doesn't work that way. If anything, it's a show about not needing boxes. There is no single, simple, tidy, easily digestible message. It's a weave of a great many things and dimensions, like in life."

Comer: "What excites me most about this show is that every viewer can see something different in it. It's so exciting to hear 20 different opinions about the same episode. That's what keeps the show fresh."

Oh: "For some viewers it's a spy thriller, others see it as a parable about modern womanhood, and for many it's something smaller altogether, about growing up and dealing with changes in life. If I had to find something in common, I'd say it's a show about obsession. You see it in all the characters, and that's what viewers connect to.

"Obsession is a terrible emotion, but I think every person experiences it. I think it's very human to be obsessed with something. Because it's an important component of the show's DNA, it manages to appeal to a very broad audience and touch viewers in deep places they may not have known they had."

* * *

Scolding journalists and grading the quality of their questions are hobbies of Oh's, and they help her cultivate an intellectual, almost intimidating air. She likes to open her answers with "that's a really good question" or "I don't like that question at all," reprimands journalists who talk over one another, and isn't afraid to say that "I've noticed the men here aren't letting their female colleagues speak, and maybe that means there are too many men here."

Perhaps the strict vibe is really the way Oh, 47 (who looks a good deal younger, if I may), savors the superstar status the show has brought her over the past year. She was born in Canada to Korean immigrants who were not especially fond of the fact that their daughter was drawn to acting. What irritated them most was her decision to give up a scholarship to study journalism at university in favor of drama studies in Montreal.

Her gamble paid off in a big way. For the past 30 years she has worked almost without pause in the American entertainment industry, with guest roles in countless series and films, from The Princess Diaries to Sideways and Dancing at the Blue Iguana.

But her biggest role until last year, the one that brought her into every home in America in 2005, was Cristina Yang in Grey's Anatomy. She played it for 12 years, collecting a clutch of awards and nominations along the way (including one Golden Globe and five Emmy nominations), and earned plenty of respect in her native Canada, including a star in her name on Canada's Walk of Fame.

But it seems all of that was just preparation for the frenzy that arrived with Killing Eve (already renewed this week for a third season), which catapulted Oh into the league of the truly big. Besides a second Golden Globe win (a record for an actress of Asian descent), a Screen Actors Guild award and Emmy and BAFTA nominations, she is getting compliments from the industry's most important critics, speaking at major events unrelated to the entertainment business, and getting to front the hosting of big ceremonies, like the last Golden Globes (the first Asian woman chosen for the job).

Oh was shrewd enough to use the moment the instant it went viral, when she took on, live on air, the racism rife in Hollywood and producers' fondness for casting white actresses in roles of women of other ethnic backgrounds. The hype around her is so big that the current rumor claims she is a candidate to be the female version of James Bond.

Oh, by the way, doesn't like that rumor. "Obviously, people say I'm suited to be Bond because I play a British intelligence agent. But no, we're unique, you can't compare Eve to some familiar male character. I understand that people like to compare, but I didn't come to do James Bond, and that's not what I want to do. I came to play women."

Oh is divorced, with no children. Her ex-husband is the director Alexander Payne, whom she met when he directed her in Sideways. She has lived in Los Angeles since the start of her career, though she received American citizenship only last year. Not long ago she celebrated the first "birthday" of her citizenship by hosting Saturday Night Live, and used the opportunity to take a few digs at Americans, with groaners like "now that I'm one of you, I'm allowed to be full of myself."

**How does it feel to receive so many awards?**

"That's a hard question, because how can you really answer it? It's wonderful to receive awards. The last awards season was really something special, but I'd say not just for me, for the show.

"I think maybe what you meant to ask was about the feeling of winning. Because I think about that a lot, whether I feel like a winner. I've come to the conclusion that I do, but the win here is collective. The show as a whole succeeded in its goal, and the awards are the expression of that."

**The show is based on a British book series, most of the actors and writers are British, and it's filmed in London. Did you feel you were bringing the American representation?**

"Not at all. I don't think the show has a distinctly British voice. Its vibe is very global, which is something I really loved. If anything, you could say the show has a female voice far more than a British one.

"Eve has a real life. She may be an agent at an intelligence agency, but she still has to deal with everyday life, like buying milk at the corner store or going to the bathroom or fighting with her husband. That gives her depth. I really loved the direction they took her character.

"In the first season you see her start to ask herself who she really is. In the second season she goes somewhere much darker. Why is she so obsessed with Villanelle? She's a middle-aged woman drawn to places of violence, who starts thinking differently about her sexuality, about her power.

"I discovered that this character is very challenging to play, especially when she really gets to dark places. I needed a lot of support and a lot of work with professionals. I went through, and am going through, a lot of psychological therapy to help me cope with this character."

* * *

While Sandra Oh takes the show pretty hard, her partner projects a lighter attitude, as in the show. "I'm not in therapy and I don't feel the need for it. If anything, the character of Villanelle is my therapy," she laughs. "I play her with a lot of joy and release so much through her. She's fun. I get a real buzz from filming her scenes."

For Comer, 26, this is a big breakthrough too. She was born and raised in Liverpool to a middle-class family; her father is a sports masseur and her mother works for the local transport authority. She still has a heavy Liverpool accent, which is rather surprising to hear ("it's funny, because it's the only accent I don't do on the show").

She started acting in plays back in primary school, and arrived in the world of television at 15, with a role in the medical soap The Royal Today. From there she worked practically without pause, and over the years landed more and more significant roles, including leads in the series Doctor Foster, Thirteen, the acclaimed miniseries The White Queen and the Morrissey biopic England Is Mine. Killing Eve finally brought her to the rank of star.

Comer is single and, she says, lives with her parents in Liverpool ("it keeps me grounded"), even though for most of the year she is filming in London.

"This show changed my life. Viewers come up to me all the time and ask to thank me for what we're doing. People tell me every day that they found themselves cheering for Villanelle, even though she commits murders and unforgivable acts. That's part of the beauty of the show, that there's no black and white, that Villanelle and Eve aren't the good and the bad in the conventional sense. You find that you love them both."

**Your character is called a psychopath. Isn't that insulting?**

She laughs. "I was instructed to make sure Villanelle was two things: a strange woman and someone who really enjoys what she does. So I could totally identify with the label psychopath."

**The media has lately been crowning you a sex symbol. You get a lot of attention from the gay community, because Villanelle is at least bisexual.**

"I don't think she's sexual in any special way, or at least not in the way we've been used to seeing from women like her on screen in the past. She's just her. We never discussed on the show what she is and what her orientation is. She lives her life, and that's fine. It makes me very happy that the audience sees something sexual in her, that they accept her."

Sandra Oh backs her up. "When a person feels comfortable in their own skin, it attracts others. Jodie's sexuality doesn't come from magazines or from a bunch of men telling her what she has to do to be sexy. Her sex appeal comes from her ease. I think a lot of women can identify with her sexual fluidity.

"In that sense, it's clearly a show created by women, starring women, telling the story of a relationship between women. Villanelle's sexuality was never discussed. We simply moved on."

**So the show does convey some message about sexuality and accepting it?**

"You're tricky, you keep trying to make me put up frames," Oh scolds me again. "Don't say message, say motif. Yes, you could say sexuality and the discovery of its limits are a motif of ours. It's something a lot of people can identify with. There's a lot of fluidity in the world, there's no need to put quotation marks around anything. The world isn't built that way. It's wonderful if what we do reaches people who until now felt unrepresented, but we weren't aiming for that."

* * *

Fashion is a big part of the show. Quite a few respected newspapers and magazines, Vanity Fair and Rolling Stone among them, have crowned Comer the fashion icon of the year.

"It's very flattering," she says, "but I can't take credit for it. There's a whole team that takes care of choosing what I wear and how I look. I do feel my opinion is very much valued. There's a bit of me in the choice of Villanelle's wardrobe, I won't deny it."

**Which look did you love most last season?**

"There were a lot, but my favorite was the suit she wears in Berlin, when she murders Bill, Eve's partner. It's a Dries Van Noten suit, very graphic. There's something about the feeling it gave me, it's very empowering."

Most of the physically demanding scenes fall on Comer. "I finish most shooting days with a few bruises here and there," she smiles, "but I have an amazing stunt double named Jessica, and she's the one who makes me look cool.

"If anything, the hardest thing for me is sustaining the energy, because we shoot a single scene with lots of breaks. For example, we shoot four seconds of a scene, stop for an hour, then come back to shoot a few more seconds. It's very hard to pick up from the point where you stopped, to be in the same frenzy and at the same level of energy, so that it feels like a natural continuation."

**Did you train before filming?**

"I didn't do fitness training, but I worked on what I call 'choreography.' Before every complex scene there are a few hard days of rehearsing the physical elements, and as far as I'm concerned they're dance days.

"The action scenes are very complex, partly because of the number of people involved in them. On screen it looks like it's just Villanelle and her victim of the week, but in practice there are loads of people working to make the blood spurt exactly the way it should," she laughs.

**Is it hard to work with so much fake blood?**

"It's actually very tasty, you know? I found out they make it from sugar, so what you see spurting on me is a kind of pure liquid sugar. I don't eat it, don't write that! But if it gets into my mouth, and that happens quite a lot, I'm not complaining."

**What was your favorite murder last season?**

"Hmm, there were a lot of good murders," she laughs. "I think the most dramatic murder was Bill's. It wasn't the most creative murder, she stabbed him with a knife in a club, but it was so shocking in terms of the story that it resonated with me the most. Bill was a main character, and his elimination left viewers in shock. At that point the viewer understood that Villanelle has no limits, and more than that, that the show itself has no limits: any character can die at any stage. It keeps viewers on the edge of their seats."

Sandra Oh: "Last season ended with Eve stabbing Villanelle in her apartment, which for me is like a thunderclap. It's a lot more than just a stabbing. There's something symbolic in it, of penetration. They finally enter each other's orbit, invade each other's personal space, and the result is a volcano.

"There was a lot of action in the finale scene, but for me the dialogue is the most significant part. They say they think about each other all the time. They are essentially declaring their obsession, which is a kind of love. The second season opens with them far more united; now they literally share blood."

Comer: "I think that event changed the way the characters see reality. Villanelle is used to being in control. This season you see her dealing with the loss of that."

**With all the love, let's be honest, during filming it doesn't look like you meet much.**

"A lot of scenes really are filmed separately," says Comer. "We know the other one is working on something, but only when it reaches the screen do we see the final result. It's interesting to discover it along with the viewers. I finally understand what she was doing all that time.

"This season they do meet. Further on there will be a lot of interactions. Two different worlds will finally connect."
