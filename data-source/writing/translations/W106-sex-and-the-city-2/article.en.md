**''Sex and the City'',** USA, 2010

*Watch the trailer for "Sex and the City 2"*

Unfortunately, we still have no way of giving a trash rating. Yellow stars, maybe? We'll think about it for next time. The reason the film "Sex and the City 2," which opened in Israel last night (Thursday), gets a relatively high rating from us is not, heaven forbid, because it's a work by Fellini, but because it is a thoroughly trashy product. And as such, it does the job wonderfully.

The new film in the series about the four girls from New York is silly, shallow, plotless and almost without pretensions (although it does have a few important messages, and more on that below). It is fun, addictive, sweeping and attractive. Or as a good friend put it on the way out of the theater: "It was insanely long, packed with ridiculous looks and exhausting dialogue, and you leave feeling that everyone is terribly rich except you. Can't wait for the third one!"

So after we enjoyed ourselves, laughed, envied and said out loud "what is this nonsense," we bring you our impressions of the ultimate girls' film, divided into categories. Carrie, over to you:

## Fashion

Apparently the film's leading star. The fashion in "Sex and the City 2" is excessive and horrifyingly expensive. Before the film's release, various rumors spread online about the plot. According to one especially interesting version, Big fails in business and he and Carrie drop dramatically in their standard of living and go through a crisis of trust. That could have created credibility and brought the audience closer to the characters, but it didn't happen. Carrie enjoys, in the apartment she shares with Big, a large, designed walk-in closet stuffed with luxury items, and it is infuriating. Naturally she also keeps another packed closet in her previous apartment. The outfits in the film are always lavish, detail-laden, grandiose and creative. The levels of ornament and preening almost recall those of the French aristocracy before the age of the guillotine. The exaggerated heels appear constantly, even when Carrie spends a quiet evening at home with Big. Most of the time there is a sense that Patricia Field is using the four characters as shop-window mannequins and simply swapping their sets at a dizzying pace to show off what she can do. And that's fine. This really is Field's film no less than Sarah Jessica Parker's. Field is a major factor sending the audience to buy tickets at the box office, and she does work here that is no less important than the screenwriter's, and even equals it. The clothes sometimes take over the plot and play an especially large part in it. The tuxedo suit Carrie wears to Stanford and Anthony's wedding, for example, draws as much attention as the event itself. On the whole, the film delivers the goods. Field keeps showing daring, pairing a puffy purple crinoline skirt with an everyday printed T-shirt, and in general creating mixes that blow your mind. The color, as always, is explosive and invigorating, and in every scene the clothes clear a new bar of sexiness and formulate female sex appeal all over again. The statement of the styling aims at a strong, prominent, sexual, energetic femininity. Patricia Field has done it again.

## Plot

Almost none. That is, there is one, but it can pretty much be summed up in a sentence and a half: the gang of girls travels to Abu Dhabi and gets up to mischief. Before, after and along the way there are a few more happenings: Stanford and Anthony marry in a glittering gay wedding, Big and Carrie work on their relationship, Charlotte copes with motherhood, Samantha with aging and Miranda with difficulties at work. But a real plot, a story-shaped one with a beginning-middle-end, there isn't. Instead, it seems that this time the emphasis is more on the small stories, without some tear-jerking dramatic climax. And that's perfectly fine, because the lives of all of us aren't a wild Hollywood plot either.

## Humor

More than the previous film, maybe more than the entire series, "Sex and the City 2" is simply a comedy. This is an insanely funny film, with a few unforgettable comic moments. The undisputed queen of the field is of course Samantha, who gets all the good jokes. The friend beside me couldn't stop laughing at her running Suzanne Somers joke (she worships her and her war on age), I cried at her "Paula Abdul" joke (one of the girls' servants in Abu Dhabi is named Abdul. And he's gay, almost like Paula), and another friend laughed terribly at Samantha's confrontation with the Arab men (condoms fall out of her bag in the middle of a bustling market). In any case, Samantha is the star of the field and gives the film an enormous comic volume.

## Sex

There is some, but not to extremes. Again, Samantha is the star of the field and the main party responsible for importing galloping male backsides into the frames. It seems they calmed down a little in this film, but still, the pound of bare flesh is supplied.

## The Big City (or "New York")

Oh, sister, where are you? The series' fifth star, the city of New York, is somewhat neglected in the film in question. The girls, as mentioned, spend most of their time in Abu Dhabi, which may be more interesting and novel, but takes away our shopping on Fifth Avenue and walks in Central Park. Whoever comes just for the serving of Manhattan will leave disappointed. Girlfriend, we miss you.

## Product Placement

As usual, a lot. Maybe even too much. At a certain point during the film I longed for a little clicker I could hold in my hand and click every time another product was shamelessly promoted. From watches through cars to computers, an entire parade of products dances its way across the length and breadth of the screen. In a consumerist series like this, the question arises of what is paid product placement and what is really part of the plot. Did the Abu Dhabi authorities put money on the enormous tourism service the film does for them? Did Suzanne Somers pay for the entire film treating her and her books as the guru of aging women? Not to mention the fashion brands, like, for example, the fact that Carrie sashays around in a shirt with "Adore Dior" written on it in huge letters. And still, since the joy of consumption is an inseparable part of the magic of "Sex and the City," the product placement is received with forgiveness. Not like in "Prisoners of War," say.

## Gay Men (and Some Lesbians Too)

Perhaps more than ever, "Sex and the City 2" appeals strongly to the gay community. The film opens with a strictly gay wedding, continues with a camp Arab pet and ends with a lesbian gesture. Along the way there is also a guest appearance by Liza Minnelli singing "Single Ladies" in what will probably be considered the gayest moment in the history of cinema. Get ready for the pride parade.

## The Silence of the Women

As is his way, Darren Star, producer and creator of the entire series, aims to make general statements about the current state of women, besides breaking creativity records in the styling. This time there are two prominent subjects treated in the plot from different angles: female silence and betrayals. Silences: the heroines travel as far as Abu Dhabi and see women whose mouths, like most of their surface area, are blocked by veils. This comes after Samantha is constantly silenced by the senior, portly partner at the firm where she works. He simply brings his hand to her mouth, without even looking at her, when she starts to speak. The scathing review, in the New Yorker, of the new book Carrie publishes comes with a cartoon in which Carrie is drawn with a plaster taped over her mouth. The message is that when it comes to marriage, the subject of Carrie's new book, she'd better keep quiet. In Abu Dhabi they keep trying to silence Samantha and rein in her sexuality and her free, confident behavior. Contradictorily, this is actually the first opportunity to see Carrie talking too much. In the series' episodes and in the first film, she inspired identification. This time less so. We meet her after she has managed to reach the longed-for reciprocity with Big and to receive his devotion. And yet she doesn't stop griping. She lets loose the most banal female complaints (the television interests you more than I do!) and sails into demanding harangues at a tired Big (why don't we go out? I'm not willing to fall asleep with you in front of the television). The final message is, as always, not black and white but complex: a place where women's mouths are shut is bad, but if you babble too much about relationships instead of growing up and seeing the other side and not only yourself, you'd better keep quiet for a bit.

## Age

The common claim from the film's fans is that the magic has weakened because the stars already look wrinkled and try too hard. There's something to it. The characters are already past 45, Samantha has even passed 50 and complains ceaselessly about the side effects of menopause. But they are still dolled up like 20-year-olds going out to the big city's clubs in search of toned backsides. Unfortunately, this reflects the Western society the characters live in. The one in which a 50-year-old and a 20-year-old can buy the same tight mini dress, if they train at the same Pilates studio. As indeed happens in the film. In the sequel, "Sex and the City 3" (it has to come. There's no reason to stop. The loyal audience's friendship with the characters is already too deep and intimate and there's no reason to cut the connection. Viewers will keep complaining that the films are shallow and keep buying tickets to them), it would be refreshing to see the producers let the characters age at their ease. Without cramming their tits into cheeky cleavages and straining their over-experienced legs on sharp heels. It would be wonderful to see the four of them relaxed in light cotton trousers with a straight cut, loafers, a thin silk button-down shirt, a delicate gold belt and a caressing cardigan. And above all it would be interesting to go another stage with the characters and examine their lives and decisions from the perspective of taking stock.

## Betrayals

Charlotte's dreams betray her. She made superhuman efforts to become a mother and it is harder and less enjoyable than she thought. Carrie fears a betrayal by Big, since they have no children and all the sparks in their lives are supposed to come from each other; Charlotte fears her nanny will cause the breakup of her marriage; Stanford and Anthony announce at their own wedding that Anthony is allowed to cheat in each of the 45 states where same-sex marriage is not legal; a fan of Carrie's feels that Carrie's decision not to bring children into the world is a betrayal of the image she has of the admired author; and there is a discussion of whether a kiss with an ex-boyfriend is a betrayal or not. The winner, of course, is female friendship. The one no nanny with arousing breasts can defeat, the one the daily routine at home won't destroy. The friends are in fact the perfect lovers. They will always be there for you, loving and available for shared indulgences. The hard work, the film reminds us again and again, is the romantic relationship. The characters are not yet mature enough to manage it, and anyone who suffers from a similar childishness is a little soldier who will help this film succeed all over the world.
