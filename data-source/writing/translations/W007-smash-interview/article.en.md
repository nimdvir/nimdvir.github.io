*Megan Hilty and Katharine McPhee, the stars of the musical drama Smash, fight over the role of Marilyn Monroe on the show and are good friends in real life. "Theater is a scandalous world where everyone is on top of everyone else all the time. All that closeness is where the action comes from," they say of the feud rumors*

*English translation of the Hebrew original by Nimrod Dvir, published in Ynet on May 1, 2013. [Read the original in Hebrew](https://www.ynet.co.il/article/4362943).*

If you missed the first season of Smash, NBC's musical, trashy, addictive series (shown in Israel on HOT3), chances are you're not quite aware of the world war that drove the plot. The show, set backstage at a Broadway musical in the making about the life of Marilyn Monroe, gave us a juicy peek into the world of musical theater.

The best show in town is in the hands of Anjelica Huston (as the producer who holds the purse strings), Debra Messing and Christian Borle (the writers) and Jack Davenport, who plays the director. Oh, and there are also two leading ladies fighting over the spotlight: Megan Hilty and Katharine McPhee. "We're very close friends," Hilty tells Ynet. "I get asked about the rivalry between us all the time, but we're not the characters we play. Our characters, Karen and Ivy, will always have a complicated relationship, but the two of us are friends like a pair of 12-year-old girls."

**Come on. The rivalry on the show is pretty extreme. It has to spill into real life.**

**Hilty:** "Not at all. Honestly, I've never known two people who were pitted against each other that intensely. But I don't think it's unrealistic. On Broadway, where you see each other in audition rooms all the time, you know who's up for the same parts and who's against you. That kind of head-to-head can happen."

**McPhee:** "Look, I'm known as a backstabbing bitch," she laughs. "But the truth is, in the world I come from there's no rivalry like that. I'm from Los Angeles, where you go to auditions and don't know a soul. Broadway feels more like a community, everyone knows everyone, it's a relatively small group. In Hollywood the competition is more boring because nobody knows anybody personally, at least until you get to the level of the big names."

If you still have no idea what they're talking about, here's the short version. Hilty plays Ivy Lynn, a wily Broadway veteran and a natural blonde who is convinced Marilyn Monroe is some kind of spiritual mother to her. In the other corner is Karen Cartwright (Katharine McPhee), a good girl from Iowa, a rookie in the theater world and, heaven help us, a brunette, with no use for cleavage or sex appeal, but when she opens her mouth to sing, angels swoon and drop out of the sky.

*The cast*

We meet on the set of the second season. As befits a prime-time show with Steven Spielberg among its producers, the money practically drips from the walls. Hilty and McPhee sit down in one of the rehearsal rooms looking wiped out, the product of shoot-rehearse-record and around again. Broadway, you know.

**One criticism of the show is that it gets Broadway wrong.**

**Hilty:** "Some people complain nonstop, but we're making a drama, not a reality show. It's entertainment. Nobody is claiming to expose Broadway's dark secrets. Still, people always ask me how realistic the show is, and I tell them that in my opinion you could go even further and still be authentic. Theater is a scandalous world. You see each other every day, sometimes twice a day, you get dressed together, you get undressed together, you sit in tiny little rooms, so everyone is terribly close all the time. All that closeness is where the action comes from."

Unlike Hilty, McPhee is a total stranger to the Broadway world, and if it were up to her she'd stay outside it. "I would never play Marilyn Monroe in real life," she admits. "I'll leave that to Megan. I'm not dying to do it, and I don't think I've got it, either. I don't have her body or her look. There's no way I'd take the part away from Michelle Williams. I would never cast myself."

McPhee likes to laugh at herself and sell herself short, pointing out, for instance, that of the whole cast she's the only one who showed up without an Emmy or a Tony on the shelf. Which moves me to compliment her on her work. "Thank you," she smiles. "I do think I manage to capture the essence of Marilyn, who she was on the inside, but a lot of the time I'm too hard on myself. As an actor, the product is you, and you feel like people are judging your soul. It's a brutal business with 95 percent unemployment, but when it works it's the most wonderful thing in the world."

McPhee is being modest, but it's hard to forget that so far her career has been one success after another. True, she didn't win American Idol, but look at Jennifer Hudson, another Idol castoff who didn't take the prize, has built an impressive career since, and is even appearing in the current season of Smash. "Honestly, we never talked about American Idol at all," McPhee says of her rehearsal-room partner. "It just didn't come up. She arrived focused and came to work. She was nice, but she kept to her corner a lot. Mostly she was surprised by how fast everything moves on this production."

*Megan Hilty. I come from the stage*

The fast pace is no accident. It's the direct result of an effort to inject some youth into the show's second season. To pull in an audience and lower the plot's average age, this season brought in a batch of young, hormonal cast members who are putting on another new musical, Hit List, built on the rock model of the feisty Rent or Spring Awakening, and even saddled Karen with a steamy romance with Jimmy (Jeremy Jordan), a hunky, tormented songwriter. And since there's nobody left in town who doesn't watch Girls, Smash too moved large chunks of the plot to young Brooklyn at the expense of more grown-up Manhattan.

**Katharine, your character is in Brooklyn a lot now, thanks to her romance with penniless Jimmy, who lives there.**

"That really is a problem, because I can't stand him," she laughs. "The truth is there was no electricity between us at the start, and that is a problem, because I'm a big believer that you can't fake chemistry. Luckily, our chemistry built up over the season, and it looks real, because on the show too the relationship starts off limping and then builds."

The show's future is up in the air right now, especially given the not exactly flattering reviews it's taking from every direction. As far as McPhee and Hilty are concerned, they've already won. After all, Steven Spielberg is their mentor. "Steven is such a nice guy," they purr. "Every time he comes to the set there's this positive buzz of excitement. He's very involved. It's a great honor to work with him."

**Honor's nice, but another season isn't a given. What will you do if the show goes off the air?**

**Hilty:** "I've just finished recording a new album that's coming out soon, and you'll see me on Broadway stages too."

**McPhee:** "I'd love to do a play someday, but not a musical. I've done so many musical numbers on this show. We perform every song in an episode about 30 times, so once it's over I'd rather do something else. Right now I want to focus on television. They say you should save your youth for the camera, so that's what I'm doing. The stage can wait."
