*A young man who killed himself on a live stream, and thousands of users who remained indifferent, left Nimrod Dvir wondering what has come over this generation*

*English translation of the Hebrew original by Nimrod Dvir, published in Ynet on November 26, 2008. [Read the original in Hebrew](https://www.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-3628359,00.html).*

Last week a tragedy happened. A 19-year-old from Florida, Abraham Biggs by name, ended his life by taking pills. Unlike others who choose to finish things in private, Biggs made use of a common internet service, lifecasting, the online broadcast of slices of life. Biggs broadcast his "lifecasting" (or more accurately, "deathcasting") on the website Justin.tv in front of dozens of excited users. The act was even accompanied by a kind of promo, since a few hours earlier Biggs had announced that he was planning to carry out a public suicide.

During the act itself, in which, as noted, Biggs took pills on a live stream, a heated argument ran in parallel among the users: some claimed he was faking, some cheerfully egged him on, and some, really only a small few, tried to talk him out of it. The end was grim: one of the users notified the forum administrator, the police intervened, but for Biggs it was already too late.

There's no point making self-righteous complaints against the internet. To go on about the internet being a place without borders would be tiresome, since that is the whole purpose of the net. This is not the first time someone has killed himself on a live stream, and records for online shock are broken every day. What is really interesting here is the reaction of the audience, which according to later reports was made up almost entirely of teenagers and twenty-somethings.

The very fact that the suicide was received with relative calm, without too many raised eyebrows, shows how much our generation, as young people who grew up with the internet hooked up to a vein, takes the matter of reality as a given. We

are used to seeing Einav Bubil faint on live television, or in the United States (and surely soon here too): stabbings, sex-change surgeries, betrayals, everything live on tape. After a whole life of growing up inside the reality bubble, it's no wonder that when someone kills himself right in your face, the question "is this real" or "is this immoral" becomes harder.

I have nothing against reality TV. There's no getting around it: reality is what defines the new generation (as you can see on our cover too). But we have to admit the painful implication of the matter: in a world without borders, where everything is broadcastable and broadcast, the line between reality, tragedy and entertainment becomes too thin.
