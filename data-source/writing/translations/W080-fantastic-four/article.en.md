*A scientist, a rock man, a human torch and an invisible woman join forces to save the world. It may sound like the start of a joke, but don't be confused: this is the summer comic-book movie, Fantastic Four. Nimrod Dvir met the foursome and looked into how they keep their figures, why they all suffer from inferiority complexes and why one of them needed psychiatric treatment*

*English translation of the Hebrew original by Nimrod Dvir, published in Ynet on July 13, 2005. [Read the original in Hebrew](https://www.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-3112281,00.html).*

**Nimrod Dvir, New York**

<!-- Source image not embedded; see source-record.json for provenance and licensing. -->

*Fantastic Four*

Female entertainment journalists, especially the hard-bitten ones, may be the most hilarious and bitter species of humanity on the planet: not only do they have to spend all day interviewing anorexics and hunks, afterward they have to go home and drool fictitious drool over them at the keyboard. As fate would have it, the interview with the stars of Fantastic Four took place in a room full of such grumblers. In honor of the film, a fine summer action flick about four superheroes who work as a team, a floor was closed off at the Essex House hotel on Central Park and a rotation of the actors was organized between the journalists' rooms. Me, it turns out, they seated as the fifth wheel beside four entertainment bitches, a sort of big-haired aunties from around the globe just itching to tear Jessica Alba to shreds. It took a second to realize I was in the best room in the house: "I'm telling you, we got screwed," Tina from Taiwan began, breaking the ice, "because we're in the morning we only get coffee and a muffin. If we were in the afternoon they'd give us pasta." That was it: from the moment we switched to the language of freeloaders we were officially open, and that's how I got to know my table-mates: Tina from Taiwan, Dorothy from France, Linda from Britain and Angelica from Italy, hereinafter "the Fantastic Four." And why is that a prize? Because not only did the wicked aunties laugh at the actors to their faces, a second after the star of the hour left the room they got busy tearing him to shreds with juicy gossip. The best action, in biddy juice.

## The Shy One

First into the room is Ioan Gruffudd, 32, who plays Mr. Fantastic, a scientist who turns into a rubber man. Contrary to the nerdy look in the film, Gruffudd turned out to be the bonbon of the cast, and with a bit of gel in his hair and a black suit managed to get the aunties to release a sigh after every answer.

**In the film you're a sort of dysfunctional family. Were relations on set tighter?**

"Of course, we'd go out to dinners, bowling, things like that. It wouldn't work if we weren't friends, I think you can see the good chemistry between us on screen."

**So it was nice getting up for work?**

"It's fun to see the final product, but the process itself was simply exhausting. Obviously I can't physically stretch, so the whole shoot I was busy imagining. You have to think about perspective, how it'll look afterward, it's pretty weird."

**In the film you're a chief scientist, any connection to real life?**

"The truth is I hate science, I failed math at school. I just can't take it in. (Tina, to the table: "Me too!") I liked the fact that he's oblivious and unaware of his abilities. He goes through some kind of journey in the film, discovers the hero inside him. In general, I've often gotten to play heroes, it's something I really love doing."

Suddenly the aunties fall silent, since there's a chance the guy is showing off.

**"So you take yourself for a hero?",**

asks Linda from Britain apprehensively. "No, no," Gruffudd pulls himself together, "I don't think I'm a hero. The real heroes are the doctors, the police and so on. The teachers, yes, the teachers! Those are the real heroes."

Dorothy, falling in love again:

**"You've got a good body in the film, did you train a lot for the role?".**

"No training at all," the actor assures us, "I wore a muscle suit under the costume to define the body. Chris Evans, the Human Torch, is a real hunk. I needed a little help."

And what do the biddies say? D. from France: "Just let me say one thing: meow!" L. from Britain: "He's in the trying-hard phase now, that's why he's so nice. Two more films and he'll be a diva like everyone else." A. from Italy: "Did you see the jacket he was wearing? That's Armani, I like." T. from Taiwan: "He's going to marry his girlfriend Alice, right? She should say thank you every day, the bitch."

## The Spiritual One

If Gruffudd was the high, Chris Evans (Cellular), of all people, counts as the low. The 24-year-old, who plays "the Human Torch," may occupy the hunk slot in the film, but in reality he turns out to be an introverted kid with scruffy facial hair. The aunties immediately go on the attack:

**Why are you naked the whole film?**

"They're always taking my shirt off, telling me it doesn't hurt if I show a bit of body. It's embarrassing, I feel like a model. I had to train for six months for this body, but I'm kind of a gym bunny anyway, so it wasn't too hard."

**What did you like about the character?**

"Johnny is an open guy, lives in the moment. Unlike him, I'm terribly self-conscious, at levels of serious insecurity. I'm better with myself now, with the film's success and my future projects, like a Danny Boyle film. And yet, I still always think about how it could all disappear fast, because that's the thing about this business, you're never really 'there.' It's fine, in any case my goal in life isn't to conquer the acting world, but to conquer a much more spiritual summit." Ignoring Tina's vomiting faces, he goes on: "I'm very into Buddhism now, I don't know how a rational person can read about it and not believe, because it's just so right."

**So why are you in the business?**

"I'm in the industry because I'm drowning in fears and insecurity, things this profession helps erase to a certain degree. My career is going great right now, but there's a chance I'll leave it all and do real research. In the meantime I'm reading books and looking for a teacher who'll help me fulfill my life's ambition: to neutralize the ego."

And to the aunties: L. from Britain, in love: "Delicious, simply delicious," versus A. from Italy: "Oh come on, did he have to come to the interview in pajamas?" D. from France sums up: "What does it matter, I'll meditate with him at any hour of the day, he only has to ask."

## The Materialist

And now the crowning glory arrives in the room, Jessica Alba, who plays the Invisible Woman in the film. Alba, barely 24, is already an old pro, with roles in Sin City, Dark Angel and more. As befits a seasoned star she pulls off a dramatic entrance while muttering "cold-in-here-cold-in-here-cold-in-here," and runs to turn off the air conditioning. The aunties, for their part, roll their eyes.

"It's a dream role," she opens with a monologue known in advance, "I'm drawn to the character's strength and confidence, to the fact that she's a strong, opinionated woman. The fact that she's a superhero is just a bonus."

**There's a scene in the film where your character finds she's plastered all over the city's newspapers. That's more or less what's happening to you now?**

"It's crazy! I walk around New York, and everywhere I look at myself. All this publicity will pass next month, I know, but that's also what's beautiful about this business: you can keep reinventing yourself."

**You've been in the business since you were 12, isn't it tiring?**

"I remember that around me kids were always going to summer camps, and I was always on film sets. It's just something I badly wanted to do, but I only recommend it if you're really suited to the business. If you're not 'driven,' with crazy willpower, you have no business here. For me, what drove me was the need for love. Until I started working I didn't fit in anywhere, even in my family I felt I didn't belong, and suddenly on set they loved me. That was enough for me to stay."

**Just love?**

"Of course not. I admit, from a very young age I wanted to have a lot of money, I just understood that I'm not one of those people who can live on a budget. I like being able not to count pennies, to buy whatever I feel like. It's not that I go shopping every day and blow 10 Louis Vuittons in one go, but I do buy nice bags now and then, things like that. Nothing to be done, without money you can't enjoy life."

Uh oh, here she's apparently managed to anger Tina from Taiwan, who for some reason decides this is the time for lunch break, pulls a packet of nachos plus dip (!) out of her bag and starts wolfing them down in front of Alba's stunned face.

**"What are your tips for staying thin?", Linda the Brit decides to exploit the opportunity presented by our Taiwanese partner turning out to be a serious beast.**

"I drink a lot of water," Alba tries to collect herself, "I do the gym and weights, I eat healthy."

**In the film you're a very maternal character. Do you feel like having a baby in real life too?**

"Oh, I really am a maternal person. But I have to keep reminding myself that it's too early, that I'd better calm down with the desire for children. I even went to a spiritual adviser, and he told me I have to take a few years and focus only on my career."

**What about school?**

"I'm not sad I didn't do it. I'd have been glad to be in an academic environment, but it would have stopped my life, because I've known what I want to do since a very young age. University prepares you for the real world, but I've been living in it since I was 12. You could say I never needed school to grow up, because I was always a little older." The biddies close the matter: A. from Italy: "I have her shirt, only in brown. I bought it at Barneys, don't get any ideas." D. from France: "Let's admit it, she's not the bombshell I thought. Cute, tops." T. from Taiwan sums up: "Fine, turn the air conditioning back on. I'm evaporating here."

## The Complainer

Last to roll into the room is Michael Chiklis, known to television viewers as Detective Mackey from the excellent series The Shield. As befits a well-equipped guy, he arrives with a blonde assistant, who sits on the side the whole interview knitting him a scarf. In the film Chiklis plays "the Thing," a strong rock man, a role that required wearing an uncomfortable latex suit with no air vents for five months.

"It was dreadful, glue on the nose, glue on the mouth, hot, heavy, you're dying to tear it off and you can't," Chiklis complains, "I'm not a claustrophobic guy, but this was already unbearable, a real personal invasion of your organs. I've never been through anything as hard as the experience of working inside that suit. Although I'm usually a balanced person, this time I had an unexpected crisis, I had to see a psychiatrist after I collapsed on set. She gave me some tools to carry on, without her I couldn't have done it."

**So why did you agree in the first place?**

"I didn't know what I was getting into, but in the end we managed. For instance, during breaks they'd take a rock out of my head and stick an air-conditioning hose inside. I also drank a lot, every day I'd put away at least 20 bottles. In the end, the suit created the character. Like 'the Thing,' I too was stuck in a body I didn't want to be in, suddenly I could better understand what drives him, why he's angry all the time."

**Would you do it again?**

"I'm so happy to be out of the suit that I can't think about it right now. What's certain is that if there's a next time we'll make some changes to the suit so I'm more comfortable. Add a zipper at the bottom, for instance." A moment before the blonde with the scarf drags him out, Chiklis finds time to deliver a message to the nation: "I was told The Shield is doing nicely in Israel. That makes me very happy because my wife is Jewish, I feel connected to the country." And what do the aunties say in summary? What summary, the second the interview ended the four competed over who'd be first out the door to smoke a cigarette. That's how it is, it's stressful being an entertainment journalist.
