*A scientist, a rock man, a human torch and an invisible woman join forces to save the world. It may sound like the setup for a joke, but make no mistake: this is the summer's comic-book movie, Fantastic Four. Nimrod Dvir met the foursome and found out how they keep their figures, why they all have inferiority complexes and why one of them needed a psychiatrist*

*Fantastic Four*

Women entertainment journalists, the hard-bitten ones especially, may be the funniest and bitterest subspecies of humanity on the planet: not only do they spend all day interviewing anorexics and hunks, they then have to go home and drool fake drool over them at the keyboard. As luck would have it, the interview with the stars of Fantastic Four took place in a room full of exactly these biddies. For the film, a fine summer action flick about four superheroes who work as a team, the studio closed off a floor of the Essex House hotel on Central Park and rotated the actors between rooms full of journalists. Me, it turned out, they had seated as a fifth wheel next to four entertainment bitches, a sort of big-haired aunties from around the globe who were just itching to tear Jessica Alba to shreds. It took me a second to realize I was in the best room in the house: "I'm telling you, we got screwed," said Tina from Taiwan, breaking the ice. "Because we're the morning group we only get coffee and a muffin. If we were the afternoon group they'd give us pasta." That was that. Once we switched to the language of freeloaders we were officially open, and I got to know my tablemates: Tina from Taiwan, Dorothy from France, Linda from Britain and Angelica from Italy, hereinafter the Fantastic Four. And why is that a prize? Because not only did the wicked aunties laugh in the actors' faces, the second the star of the hour left the room they got down to shredding him with juicy gossip. Action at its juiciest, biddy edition.

## The Shy One

First through the door is Ioan Gruffudd, 32, who plays Mr. Fantastic, a scientist who turns into a rubber man. Despite the nerd look he wears in the film, Gruffudd turned out to be the bonbon of the cast, and with a little gel in his hair and a black suit he had the aunties sighing after every answer.

**In the film you're a sort of dysfunctional family. Were you closer on set?**

"Of course. We'd go out to dinner, bowling, that kind of thing. It wouldn't have worked if we weren't friends. I think you can see the chemistry between us on screen."

**So getting up for work was a pleasure?**

"It's fun to see the finished product, but the process itself was just a grind. Obviously I can't physically stretch, so I spent the whole shoot imagining. You have to think about perspective, how it's going to look later. It's pretty weird."

**In the film you're a top scientist. Any connection to real life?**

"Honestly, I hate science. I failed math at school. I just can't get my head around it. (Tina, to the table: "Me too!") I liked that he's oblivious, that he has no idea what he's capable of. He goes on a kind of journey in the film and discovers the hero inside. I've gotten to play heroes a lot, actually. It's something I really love doing."

Suddenly the aunties fall silent. There's a chance the guy is bragging.

**"So you think of yourself as a hero?"**

asks Linda from Britain, warily. "No, no," Gruffudd says, catching himself. "I don't think I'm a hero. The real heroes are doctors, police officers and so on. Teachers, yes, teachers! Those are the real heroes."

Dorothy, falling back in love:

**"You've got a good body in the film. Did you train a lot for the role?"**

"No training at all," the actor insists. "I wore a muscle suit under the costume to give the body some definition. Chris Evans, the Human Torch, is the real hunk. I needed a little help."

And the biddies' verdict? D. from France: "Let me just say one thing: meow!" L. from Britain: "He's still in the trying-hard phase, that's why he's so nice. Two more films and he'll be a diva like the rest of them." A. from Italy: "Did you see the jacket he had on? Armani. I approve." T. from Taiwan: "He's marrying his girlfriend Alice, right? She should say thank you every single day, the bitch."

## The Spiritual One

If Gruffudd was the high, Chris Evans (Cellular) counts as the low. The 24-year-old, who plays the Human Torch, may fill the hunk slot in the film, but in person he turns out to be a withdrawn kid with scruffy facial hair. The aunties go straight on the attack:

**Why are you naked for the whole film?**

"They keep taking my shirt off. They tell me it won't hurt to show a little body. It's embarrassing, I feel like a model. I had to train for six months for this body, but I'm kind of a gym rat anyway, so it wasn't too hard."

**What did you like about the character?**

"Johnny is an open guy, he lives in the moment. I'm the opposite, terribly self-conscious, seriously insecure. I'm better with myself now, with the film doing well and the projects I've got coming, like a Danny Boyle film. And still, I'm always thinking about how fast it could all go away, because that's the thing about this business, you're never really 'there.' That's fine. My goal in life isn't to conquer the acting world anyway, it's to conquer a much more spiritual summit." Ignoring Tina's gagging faces, he goes on: "I'm really into Buddhism right now. I don't know how a reasonable person can read about it and not believe, because it's just so right."

**So why are you in the business?**

"I'm in this industry because I'm drowning in fears and insecurities, and this profession helps make them disappear, up to a point. My career is going great right now, but there's a real chance I'll drop it all and do some real searching. In the meantime I'm reading books and looking for a teacher who can help me with my life's ambition: to neutralize the ego."

Over to the aunties. L. from Britain, smitten: "Delicious, simply delicious." Versus A. from Italy: "Oh please, did he have to show up to the interview in his pajamas?" D. from France sums up: "Who cares, I'll meditate with him any hour of the day or night. He only has to ask."

## The Materialist

And here comes the main attraction, Jessica Alba, who plays the Invisible Woman. Alba, barely 24, is already an old hand, with Sin City, Dark Angel and more behind her. As befits a seasoned star, she makes a dramatic entrance muttering "it's-cold-it's-cold-it's-cold" and runs to shut off the air conditioning. The aunties, for their part, roll their eyes.

"It's a dream role," she opens, with the monologue we all saw coming. "I'm drawn to the character's strength and confidence, to the fact that she's a strong woman with opinions. The superhero part is just a bonus."

**There's a scene in the film where your character finds herself plastered across every paper in the city. Is that more or less what's happening to you right now?**

"It's crazy! I walk around New York and everywhere I look, I see myself. All this publicity will be gone next month, I know, but that's what's beautiful about this business too: you can keep reinventing yourself."

**You've been in the business since you were 12. Doesn't it wear you out?**

"I remember the kids around me always going off to summer camp, and I was always on a film set. It was just something I badly wanted to do, but I only recommend it if you're really built for this business. If you're not driven, with insane willpower, there's nothing for you here. For me, what drove me was the need for love. Until I started working I didn't fit in anywhere, I didn't even feel like I belonged in my own family, and suddenly on set they loved me. That was enough to make me stay."

**Just love?**

"Of course not. I admit it, from a very young age I wanted to have a lot of money. I just understood I'm not one of those people who can live on a budget. I like being able to not keep score, to buy whatever I feel like. It's not like I go shopping every day and blow through 10 Louis Vuittons in one go, but I do buy nice bags now and then, that kind of thing. What can you do, without money you can't enjoy life."

Uh oh. That one seems to have gotten to Tina from Taiwan, who for some reason decides it's time for lunch, pulls a bag of nachos plus dip (!) out of her purse and starts scooping away in front of Alba's stunned face.

**"What are your tips for staying thin?" Linda the Brit asks, seizing the moment now that our Taiwanese colleague has turned out to be a proper animal.**

"I drink a lot of water," Alba says, trying to collect herself. "Gym, weights, I eat healthy."

**In the film you're a very maternal character. Do you want a baby in real life too?**

"Oh, I really am a maternal person. But I have to keep reminding myself it's too early, that I should relax about wanting kids. I even went to a spiritual adviser, and he told me I have to take a few years and focus only on my career."

**What about school?**

"I'm not sad I didn't do it. I'd have loved being in a learning environment, but it would have put my life on hold, because I've known what I want to do since I was very young. College prepares you for the real world, but I've been living in it since I was 12. You could say I never needed school to grow up, because I was always a little older than my age." The biddies close the case. A. from Italy: "I have her top, only in brown. I got it at Barneys, in case you were wondering." D. from France: "Let's be honest, she's not the bombshell I thought she'd be. Cute, tops." T. from Taiwan sums up: "Fine, turn the air conditioning back on. I'm evaporating in here."

## The Complainer

Last to roll in is Michael Chiklis, known to television viewers as Detective Mackey from the excellent The Shield. As befits a well-equipped guy, he arrives with a blonde assistant, who sits off to the side for the whole interview knitting him a scarf. In the film Chiklis plays the Thing, a strongman made of rock, a role that meant five months inside an uncomfortable latex suit with no air holes.

"It was horrible. Glue on your nose, glue on your mouth, hot, heavy, you're dying to rip it off and you can't," Chiklis complains. "I'm not a claustrophobic guy, but this was unbearable, a real invasion of your own body. I've never been through anything as hard as working inside that suit. I'm usually a pretty balanced person, but this time I had a crisis I didn't see coming. I had to see a psychiatrist after I broke down on set. She gave me some tools to keep going. Without her I couldn't have done it."

**So why did you say yes in the first place?**

"I didn't know what I was getting into, but in the end we worked it out. During breaks, for instance, they'd pull a rock out of my head and stick an air-conditioning hose in. I drank a lot too, at least 20 bottles a day. In the end, the suit built the character. Like the Thing, I was stuck in a body I didn't want to be in, and suddenly I understood better what drives him, why he's angry all the time."

**Would you do it again?**

"I'm so happy to be out of that suit that I can't even think about it right now. What's for sure is that if there's a next time, we're making some changes to the suit so I'm more comfortable. A zipper at the bottom, for instance." A moment before the blonde with the scarf drags him out, Chiklis finds time for a message to the nation: "I hear The Shield is doing well in Israel. That makes me very happy, because my wife is Jewish and I feel connected to the country." And the aunties' closing verdict? What verdict. The second the interview ended, the four of them raced each other out the door for a cigarette. That's how it is. It's stressful, being an entertainment journalist.
