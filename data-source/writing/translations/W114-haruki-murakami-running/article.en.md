*What I Talk About When I Talk About Running, by Haruki Murakami, is an absorbing diary of devotion, love and the tireless pursuit of a goal. Who would have thought a book about running could be this interesting*

*English translation of the Hebrew original by Nimrod Dvir, published in nrg Maariv on August 26, 2010. [Read the original in Hebrew](https://www.makorrishon.co.il/nrg/online/47/ART2/149/265.html).*

**"Pain is inevitable. Suffering is optional."**

The well-worn insight above kept echoing in my head as I read Haruki Murakami's new book, What I Talk About When I Talk About Running (and yes, he uses the cliché too).

Murakami, the celebrated Japanese novelist who gave the literary world Norwegian Wood, After Dark and many other fine books, steps away here from his dramatic style and takes a semi-autobiographical turn. The new book, whose title is borrowed from Raymond Carver's What We Talk About When We Talk About Love, is really a personal journal in which Murakami lays out his relationship with the sporting hobby that has followed him through his life: long-distance running.

A whole book about running sounds like a fairly dull idea. But, as the book proves, when you love something (and borrowing the title from Carver shows that love is what this is) you can write about it with enough feeling, passion and interest to fill 185 dense, absorbing pages.

I am not a runner, and in fact maintain the image of a serious couch potato. And still, as I read I couldn't help sinking with Murakami into the runner's joy, the thrill of waking up in the morning and heading out, and above all into what is, for me, the book's real subject: the ability to set a goal and then go after it with everything you have.

Murakami is aiming to run the New York Marathon, 42 grueling kilometers over the bridges and down the avenues of the American city. That is the book's frame story, in effect, though of course there's no real plot here.

On the way to his goal Murakami tells us about his training, what he thinks about while running, the techniques he has developed to survive the long courses, his credo when it comes to sport, and the mental and physical difficulties he ran into along the way.

There's a fair amount of life philosophy along the way, the kind a reader who isn't a runner can connect with too. Murakami's style is restrained and informative, and yet very readable and engaging. Einat Cooper's translation from the Japanese manages to carry the author's flowing thoughts, as he tells us a story with no pretension, no attempt to preach and no particular pride. Just a book about running.

I can't say the book left me wanting to lace up and run a few laps of the neighborhood. It did leave me with a sense of ease and cheer, and above all with a great deal of respect for the author and for people like him in general, who do what they love and learn along the way about restraint, patience, devotion and the tireless pursuit of a goal, whatever it may be.

**What I Talk About When I Talk About Running, Haruki Murakami. Translated from the Japanese by Einat Cooper. Keter, 185 pages**
