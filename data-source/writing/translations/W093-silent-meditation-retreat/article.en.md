*Nimrod Dvir kept silent for 10 days, meditated, ate vegetarian food and thought good thoughts. We want to hit him, but he's in New York*

*English translation of the Hebrew original by Nimrod Dvir, published in Pnai Plus / Xnet on February 12, 2012. [Read the original in Hebrew](https://xnet.ynet.co.il/xo/articles/0,14718,L-3093217,00.html).*

There are a few experiences in New York that you can't have anywhere else. For instance, a Vipassana meditation course I did recently. On the face of it, something you could do anywhere on the globe, and yet the fact that I went through it here made it, for me, a life-changing experience.

After some hesitation (eat only vegetarian? not talk?) I decided to go for it and signed up for a course held at the Dhamma Dhara center in the town of Shelburne, Massachusetts, under the guidance of the man who revived the method and the figure behind the teaching, S. N. Goenka.

We drove there on an improvised road trip, a fairly odd bunch: Dan, an Asian graphic designer I met through the course; Melissa, a pretty, chatty yoga instructor; Tristan, a dreamy poet from Brooklyn; and Gretchen, a sixty-something professor of computer science.

This puzzling bunch drove along in the car through the snowy roads of New York, Connecticut and finally Massachusetts, in what at times reminded me of scenes from the Israeli horror film Rabies.

When we finally reached the center, I realized I had bet right. Even though this is a minimalist experience, I assumed that a place serving the yuppie residents of the wealthy states of the eastern U.S. would not allow itself to go for the scabies level.

And indeed, it was a beautiful monastery-like building, surrounded by a snowy forest with streams, lakes and walking trails. There's a shared building with the dining room, and every meditator gets a fairly large room with a roommate (like me), or a room alone if they're relatively old (like the mini-suite Gretchen was awarded). I immediately named the place "Ya'arot HaCarmel," after the spa resort back home.

After a brief introduction we dove into the work: ten days of total silence, with even a single bit of eye contact with one another forbidden. Every day we woke at four in the morning, and until nine-thirty at night we practiced Vipassana meditation in silence.

Between meditations we ate vegetarian meals (not bad) quietly in the dining room, or walked by ourselves in the forest next to the center (and that is where my only word in all ten days came out, "shit," which flew from my mouth when I fell hard into a deep pit full of snow).

The total silence meant I could even hear the sound of the snow landing softly on the trees. Wandering through the forest, between frozen ponds and snowy paths, made me understand how far I was from home. And the thoughts, they rose and surfaced in complete freedom over the ten days: just me, myself, and insights and musings that flowed without interruption like the streams outside.

Since then I feel that something significant has changed in my life. The others went through a similar experience too, and on the drive back I felt that we were all actually in a kind of high, a feeling you get after using drugs, except that here it was natural and lasted ten days.

In the time since, I've understood that life in New York itself, for me, can also be a kind of Vipassana. You can spend whole days here in silence, alone, without letting outside disturbances cut off your thoughts, which hop along between the buildings and arrange themselves in your head like Tetris blocks.

That possibility, to meditate even in the middle of everyday life, is in my eyes one of the city's true points of beauty.
