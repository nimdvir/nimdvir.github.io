*Nimrod Dvir kept quiet for ten days, meditated, ate vegetarian and thought good thoughts. We'd like to smack him, but he's in New York*

*English translation of the Hebrew original by Nimrod Dvir, published in Xnet on February 12, 2012. [Read the original in Hebrew](https://xnet.ynet.co.il/xo/articles/0,14718,L-3093217,00.html).*

New York offers a few experiences you can't have anywhere else. A Vipassana meditation course I took recently, for instance. On paper it's something you could do anywhere on the globe, and yet the fact that I went through it here made it, for me, a life-changing experience.

After some dithering (vegetarian only? no talking?) I decided to go for it and signed up for a course at the Dhamma Dhara center in the town of Shelburne, Massachusetts, under the guidance of S. N. Goenka, the man who revived the technique and the mind behind the teaching.

We drove up on an improvised road trip, a fairly odd bunch: Dan, an Asian graphic designer I met through the course; Melissa, a good-looking, chatty yoga instructor; Tristan, a dreamy poet from Brooklyn; and Gretchen, a computer science professor somewhere in her sixties.

This puzzling crew rolled along the snowy roads of New York, Connecticut and finally Massachusetts, in what at times reminded me of scenes from the Israeli horror film Rabies.

When we finally reached the center, I knew I'd bet right. Minimalist experience or not, I had assumed that a place serving the yuppies of the rich East Coast states would not let itself sink to scabies level.

And indeed: a beautiful, monastery-like building surrounded by snowy forest, with streams, lakes and walking trails. There's a communal building with the dining hall, and every meditator gets a fairly large room with a roommate (like me) or a room to themselves if they're relatively old (like the mini-suite Gretchen scored). I immediately christened the place Ya'arot HaCarmel, after the spa resort back home.

After a brief round of introductions we dove into the work: ten days of total silence, with even eye contact between us forbidden. Every day we woke at four in the morning and practiced Vipassana meditation in silence until nine-thirty at night.

Between sittings we ate vegetarian meals (not bad) in silence in the dining hall, or took ourselves for walks in the forest next door (which is where my only word in all ten days came out: "shit," which flew out of my mouth as I plunged into a deep pit full of snow).

The total silence meant I could hear even the sound of snow settling softly on the trees. Wandering the forest, between frozen ponds and snowy paths, made me realize how far from home I was. And the thoughts: they rose and surfaced in complete freedom over the ten days, just me, myself, and insights and musings that flowed uninterrupted like the streams outside.

Since then I've felt that something significant has shifted in my life. The others went through something similar, and on the drive back I felt that all of us were, in fact, on a kind of high, the feeling you get from drugs, except that here it was natural and had lasted ten days.

In the time since, I've come to understand that life in New York itself can, for me, be a kind of Vipassana too. You can spend whole days here in silence, alone, without letting outside interruptions cut off your thoughts, which skip between the buildings and settle into place in your head like Tetris blocks.

That option, meditating in the middle of ordinary life, is to my mind one of the city's real beauties.
